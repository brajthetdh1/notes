Part A — Replication Topologies Who writes, who follows, and who disagrees

1

What is replication, and why is it used?

Short Answer

Replication keeps multiple copies of the same data on different nodes. It buys durability (survive a node loss), availability (serve from survivors), read scalability (parallel reads), and low latency (serve from a nearby copy). It costs write amplification, storage, and — most importantly — consistency complexity, because keeping copies identical is a distributed coordination problem.

What replication is for

  • Durability. A single disk or machine is a single point of data loss. Three copies on independent hardware means a disk failure or a whole node failure does not lose the data. This is the most fundamental reason and the one that applies to every system.
  • Availability. If one replica is down for maintenance, upgrade, or failure, others can still serve. Without replication, any node restart is downtime.
  • Read scalability. Read-heavy workloads (typically 10:1 to 100:1 read/write) can be served by many replicas in parallel. The primary handles writes; replicas share the read load.
  • Latency. A user in Singapore served from a replica in Singapore gets a 5 ms response rather than a 200 ms round trip to Virginia. Geographic replication is the only way to fix the physics of distance.
  • Disaster recovery. A replica in a different region survives a regional outage — not just a node failure.
  • Offline and edge. Mobile apps, IoT devices, and edge nodes hold a local replica so they can operate without connectivity and synchronise later.

What replication costs

  • Write amplification. A write that touches one node now touches N. Each replica consumes network bandwidth, disk I/O, and CPU to apply the change. With replication factor 3, the total write work is three times the logical write.
  • Storage. Three copies means three times the storage cost, which is trivial for small datasets and significant for terabytes.
  • Consistency complexity. The copies can diverge. Writes may be applied in different orders, one replica may lag, and a partition may leave two replicas disagreeing. Every consistency model in Volume 3 exists because of this.
  • Operational surface. More nodes to monitor, more failure modes, more ways for things to go wrong. A lagging replica is a new operational concept that did not exist in a single-node deployment.

Replication vs backup vs sharding

These are constantly confused and the distinction matters:

MechanismWhat it doesProtects againstDoes not protect against
ReplicationKeeps live copies in syncNode failure, disk failure, region failureApplication bugs, accidental deletes (they replicate too)
BackupPeriodic point-in-time snapshotAccidental deletes, ransomware, corruptionNode failure between backups
ShardingSplits data across nodes, each holding a subsetSingle-node capacity limitsNode failure (unless each shard is replicated)

A critical point: replication is not a backup. If a user deletes a record, the delete replicates to every replica within milliseconds. The data is gone everywhere. Backups exist precisely because replication faithfully propagates mistakes.

Replication and scale

Replication solves availability and read throughput, but it does not solve write throughput — every replica still applies every write, so the write capacity is bounded by one node's write capacity (in a leader-based design) or by the coordination cost of multiple writers. Scaling writes requires partitioning (Volume 5), often combined with replication within each partition. The standard architecture is: shard the data, and replicate each shard.

Interview Framing

Say: “I'll replicate each shard with a leader and two followers — the followers give read scalability and survive a node failure, and the leader serialises writes.” That demonstrates you understand replication and sharding are complementary, not alternatives.

2

Explain leader-follower replication.

Short Answer

One node — the leader (master, primary) — accepts all writes. It records every change in a log and streams that log to followers (slaves, replicas, secondaries), which apply the changes in the same order. Reads can be served by any node. The leader is the single source of truth for write ordering, which makes consistency straightforward and failure complex.

The mechanics

  1. The client sends a write to the leader.
  2. The leader validates it, applies it to its own storage, and appends the change to its write-ahead log (WAL) or binary log.
  3. It acknowledges the write to the client — immediately (async) or after some followers confirm (sync).
  4. Followers receive the log stream, apply the changes in order, and advance their own position.
  5. Reads are served by the leader (for fresh data) or by followers (for scalable but potentially stale reads).

How followers apply the log

There are three main approaches, and the choice affects what is possible downstream:

  • Statement-based. The leader ships the actual SQL statement. Simple, but breaks on non-deterministic functions (NOW(), RAND()), auto-increment values, and triggers. MySQL's statement-based binlog mode has these pitfalls.
  • Write-ahead-log shipping. The leader ships the physical page changes. Byte-identical, but tightly coupled to the storage format and version — the follower must run the same engine version. PostgreSQL streaming replication works this way.
  • Logical / row-based. The leader ships row-level changes — insert this row, update that column. Portable across versions and usable for change data capture. This is the modern default and the basis of CDC (Question 12).

The failure process: failover

When the leader fails, the system must promote a follower. The steps are:

  1. Detect the failure — heartbeat timeout, missing keepalive, an external monitor.
  2. Elect a new leader — via a consensus protocol (Raft), a coordinator (ZooKeeper), or manual promotion (the least good option).
  3. Reconfigure — clients, proxies, and remaining followers are pointed at the new leader.
  4. Old leader rejoins — when the failed node comes back, it must be demoted and resynced from the new leader. Any writes it received before failing that were not replicated must be discarded, which is why asynchronous replication can lose data on failover.

Strengths and weaknesses

StrengthWeakness
Simple to reason about — one writer, one orderLeader is a write bottleneck and a single point of failure during failover
Supports strong consistency cleanlyFollows are asynchronous by default, so reads can be stale
Mature in every major databaseFailover takes seconds — not zero-downtime
Reads scale horizontallyWrites do not scale beyond one node's capacity

Where it is used

PostgreSQL streaming replication, MySQL replicas, MongoDB replica sets (with a primary), Kafka partitions (each partition has a leader replica), Redis Sentinel and Redis Cluster, and most managed SQL services. It is the default replication model for anything that needs serialisable writes and a well-understood failure story.

The read-your-writes problem

Because followers are asynchronous, a user who writes to the leader and then reads from a follower may see the old value — a direct violation of the user's expectations. This is the subject of Question 6, but the takeaway here is that the moment you introduce asynchronous followers, you introduce this problem and must address it at the application layer or with a session token.

Common Pitfall

Sending all reads to followers without considering freshness. A user updates their profile and immediately cannot see the change; a count seems to go backwards. Always route read-your-own-write traffic to the leader, or use a session token, or explicitly accept the staleness.

3

Explain multi-leader replication.

Short Answer

Multiple nodes accept writes and replicate to each other. This enables writes close to users in several regions and active-active availability, but it makes conflicts inevitable — two leaders can accept conflicting writes at the same time, and the system must resolve them. Multi-leader is powerful and subtle, and it is often the wrong choice unless the workload genuinely needs multi-region writes.

Why multi-leader

  • Geographic write latency. With a single leader in Virginia, a write from Singapore pays ~200 ms round trip. With a leader in each region, writes are local (5 ms) and replicate asynchronously.
  • Active-active availability. Each region can serve writes if another is unreachable. This is the classic use case for global products.
  • Offline clients. A mobile app, laptop, or edge device holds a local leader that accumulates writes and syncs when connectivity returns. The device is effectively a multi-leader replica.
  • Collaborative editing. Multiple users editing a document simultaneously are multiple writers producing concurrent changes. (Though this is usually implemented with CRDTs rather than a database multi-leader model.)

The conflict problem

Two leaders in two regions accept concurrent writes to the same row. Both succeed locally. When they replicate, they disagree. The system must resolve this. Options:

  • Last-writer-wins (LWW). Highest timestamp wins. Simple, but silently discards one user's data, and depends on clock synchronisation. This is what DynamoDB Global Tables does, and it is why conflicting writes are said to be "eventually lost".
  • Application-defined merge. The application knows the domain and can merge — e.g., a shopping cart unions both sets of items. Correct, but requires domain knowledge to be encoded.
  • CRDTs. Data types with commutative merge semantics — see Volume 3, Question 12.
  • Conflict escalation. Keep both versions, surface them to a human to resolve.

Whatever the policy, the conflict is real and the resolution loses information. There is no way to accept concurrent writes to the same row and not lose something.

Replication topologies

TopologyDescriptionIssue
StarAll leaders communicate via one central nodeCentral node is a single point of failure
RingEach leader forwards to the next in a circleA single node failure breaks the loop
All-to-allEvery leader communicates with every otherMessage ordering issues; can produce non-convergent histories

All-to-all is the most resilient but the hardest to reason about. Causal ordering can be violated when messages take different paths — a classic result is that without careful design, all-to-all topologies can leave replicas disagreeing permanently.

Multi-leader in practice

MySQL Group Replication and Percona XtraDB Cluster support multi-master mode. PostgreSQL BDR offers it. CouchDB uses a multi-leader model. DynamoDB Global Tables is the managed version. In every case the operation is materially more complex than leader-follower, and the conflicts — while rare in normal operation — are catastrophic when they occur because they involve real user data being silently overwritten.

The decision

Use multi-leader when: writes genuinely occur in multiple regions and the latency of a single leader is unacceptable, or when the data model can tolerate conflict resolution (last-write-wins for counters, CRDTs for collaborative data). Do not use it for financial ledgers, inventory, or anything where a lost write is a customer-facing failure. Most systems that think they need multi-leader actually need a single leader per region (sharding by user) — a design that avoids conflicts entirely.

Trade-off

Multi-leader trades consistency for availability and latency. When you choose it, write down the conflict resolution policy and its failure mode. “Last-write-wins” is not a resolution — it is a declaration that concurrent writes are acceptable losses. That must be a business decision, not an implementation detail.

4

Explain leaderless replication (Dynamo-style).

Short Answer

Every replica can accept both reads and writes — there is no leader. A client (or a coordinator) sends writes to N replicas and waits for W acknowledgements; reads query R replicas and take the freshest response. With R + W > N, reads are guaranteed to see the latest write. There is no failover, because there is no leader to lose — the system is always writable on whichever replicas are alive.

The write and read path

On a write, the client sends it to all replicas responsible for the key (or to a coordinator, which forwards). It waits for W responses. As long as W replicas acknowledge, the write is considered successful. On a read, the client queries R replicas; each returns the version it has; the client (or coordinator) picks the most recent. With R + W > N, at least one of the R responses overlaps with at least W of the replicas, so the latest write is visible.

Why it is attractive

  • No failover. There is no leader election, no promotion, no window of unavailability. If a replica dies, the next write simply succeeds on the remaining replicas.
  • Always writable. As long as any W replicas are alive, writes succeed. In a multi-region deployment this means every region is writable even during a partition.
  • Simple operational model. No primary to manage, no failover runbook. Add a node, it joins the ring, it starts receiving traffic.
  • Tunable consistency. The client can choose W and R per operation — ONE for speed, QUORUM for balance, ALL for maximum consistency. This makes the PACELC choice explicit and per-query.

Why it is subtle

Leaderless replication is easy to describe and easy to get subtly wrong. The issues:

  • Concurrent writes. Two clients can write different values to the same key, both reaching W replicas. No replica has a "latest" value; the system must resolve the conflict with timestamps or version vectors. This is why Cassandra and Riak support last-write-wins or per-key versioning.
  • Read repair. When a read queries R replicas and finds divergent values, it can write the latest value back to stale replicas. This repairs the inconsistency in the background — but only for keys that are actually read.
  • Anti-entropy. A background process compares replicas and repairs differences, because read repair only fixes what is read. Merkle trees (Question 9) make the comparison efficient.
  • Hinted handoff. If a replica is unreachable, another node temporarily accepts the write and delivers it when the original recovers. This maintains the write-availability guarantee at the cost of weakening quorum overlap (Question 10).
  • Stale reads. Even with R + W > N, if a replica has a partially applied write or conflicting writes, the "latest" value can be wrong. Cassandra's quirks with LWW timestamps and clock skew are well documented.

Leaderless vs leader-based

AspectLeader-basedLeaderless
Write availabilityLeader must be upW replicas must be up
FailoverRequired, takes secondsNone
ConsistencyStraightforwardTunable, requires conflict handling
ConflictsAvoided by serialising on leaderUnavoidable — must be resolved
Operational simplicityWell understood, mature toolingMore complex for the developer
ExamplesPostgreSQL, MySQL, MongoDB, KafkaCassandra, DynamoDB, Riak, Voldemort

When to use it

Leaderless fits workloads with high write availability requirements, geographically dispersed writers, and data that tolerates conflict resolution (counters, event logs, time-series, shopping carts). It is the wrong choice for financial ledgers, inventory, or anything with invariants that must hold across concurrent writers. When in doubt, the simpler leader-based model with a well-understood failover is usually the better engineering trade.

Interview Framing

Say: “I'll use Cassandra with QUORUM reads and QUORUM writes for the event log, so writes are available even when a replica is down and reads are guaranteed fresh. For the ledger I'll use a single-leader database with a synchronous standby.” That demonstrates you are choosing per data type, not per product.

What is the difference between synchronous, asynchronous, and semi-synchronous replication?

Short Answer

Synchronous — the leader waits for followers to acknowledge before replying to the client. Durable and consistent, but a slow or dead follower blocks writes. Asynchronous — the leader replies immediately; followers catch up later. Fast and tolerant of follower failure, but can lose recent writes. Semi-synchronous — the leader waits for at least one follower, balancing the two.

AspectSynchronousSemi-synchronousAsynchronous
Leader waits forAll (or a quorum of) followersAt least one followerNo follower
Client latencyHighest — round trip to slowest followerModerate — round trip to fastest followerLowest — local write only
Data loss on leader failureNonePossible for the un-acked followersPossible for any un-replicated write
Write availabilityBlocked if any follower is downBlocked if all followers are downAlways available
Typical useFinancial ledgers, complianceMost production databasesRead replicas, DR

Synchronous replication in depth

In fully synchronous mode, the leader commits only after every replica has acknowledged. This gives a zero data loss guarantee: once the client sees the write acknowledged, the data is on multiple independent machines. The cost is that the write is blocked by the slowest replica — and if any replica is down, writes stop entirely. A single slow disk on one follower can stall the entire cluster, which is why "fully synchronous" is almost never used in production.

What people call "synchronous" in practice is usually synchronous with a quorum — for example PostgreSQL's synchronous_commit = remote_write with synchronous_standby_names = 'ANY 1 (node1, node2)' waits for one of the standbys. MySQL Group Replication uses a majority quorum. This gives zero loss without a single point of blocking.

Asynchronous replication in depth

The leader acknowledges the write as soon as it has applied it locally; replication happens in the background. This is fast and always available, and it is what read replicas almost always use. The risk is straightforward: if the leader dies before the write has replicated, the write is lost — the follower that gets promoted simply does not have it. This is the source of the failover data-loss problem.

Asynchronous replication is appropriate when: the write is not critical (analytics, logs, feeds), or the application can tolerate a few seconds of loss, or the follower is in a different region where synchronous latency would be prohibitive.

Semi-synchronous as the default

Semi-synchronous replication — wait for at least one follower — is the pragmatic default. It bounds the data-loss window to writes that were in flight when the leader failed, which is usually seconds or less. It does not block on a single slow follower as long as one follower is fast. It is a real trade-off: slightly higher write latency for substantially better durability. MySQL's semi-sync, PostgreSQL's quorum commit, and MongoDB's {w: 2} write concern all provide it.

The latency arithmetic

Write latency with synchronous replication = local write time + round trip to follower + follower apply time. In one datacenter this is 1–5 ms. Across regions it is 50–150 ms. This is the reason multi-region synchronous replication is expensive, and why most systems use synchronous replication within a region and asynchronous across regions.

The failure modes

  • Blocking on a slow follower. In fully synchronous mode, one slow disk stalls all writes. Solution: quorum-based sync, or an escape hatch that falls back to async when followers are slow.
  • Lost writes on failover. In async mode, the new leader does not have the last few writes. Solution: semi-sync or quorum, or accept the data-loss window.
  • Split-brain with two leaders. If failover is triggered but the old leader is not actually dead, both may accept writes. Solution: fencing (Question 7).
Trade-off

The choice is fundamentally: do you want the write to be durable before acknowledging (sync) or fast and available (async)? Every production system sits somewhere on this spectrum, and the exact setting should be chosen per data type. A payment needs quorum sync; a page view count does not.

6

What is replication lag, and how do you handle the read-your-writes problem it creates?

Short Answer

Replication lag is the delay between a write being committed on the leader and it being applied on a follower. It is normally milliseconds but can grow to seconds or minutes during load, network issues, or a follower restart. The user-visible consequence is that a user can write and then read stale data from a follower — a violation of read-your-writes (Volume 3, Question 7). The solutions are routing, version tokens, or waiting for the follower to catch up.

Why lag happens

  • Network delay. The log stream takes time to travel, especially across regions.
  • Follower is slow. Under-provisioned follower CPU, slow disk, competing workloads. Applying changes serially on a single-threaded applier is a common bottleneck.
  • Burst load. A large write burst on the leader overwhelms the follower's apply rate. The lag grows during the burst and drains afterwards.
  • Follower restart. A follower that has been down must catch up, which can take minutes for a large delta. During that time, reads served from it are stale.
  • Long-running queries. On the follower, a slow read can hold a snapshot and prevent the apply process from advancing (a problem in MySQL, less so in PostgreSQL).
  • Schema changes. Some DDL blocks replication, causing a lag spike during migrations.

The anomalies lag causes

  • Read-your-writes violation. A user posts, then refreshes and does not see the post because the follower has not applied it.
  • Monotonic read violation. A user reads value A from a fast follower, refreshes, and reads the older value B from a lagged one. The UI seems to go backwards.
  • Non-causal reads. A user sees a comment before the post it replies to, because the comment was applied to one follower and the post to another.

Solutions, in increasing complexity

  1. Route write-adjacent reads to the leader. For a short window after a write — or by attaching a flag to the session — serve reads from the leader. Simple and effective, but concentrates read load on the leader.
  2. Use a version token. The write returns a version (LSN, GTID, logical timestamp). The read includes it. The follower responds only if its version is ≥ the token, otherwise the request is routed to a more current replica or the leader. This is the most precise solution and is used by MongoDB causal sessions and Cosmos DB session consistency.
  3. Wait for a specific position. Related to the version token — the follower blocks briefly until it has replayed to a given position, then serves the read. Bounded wait with a timeout.
  4. Sticky sessions to the leader. Route all of a user's traffic to the leader for a short period after a write. Simpler than version tokens but coarser.
  5. Optimistic local echo. The client stores the write locally, shows it immediately, and reconciles when the server response arrives. Masks the problem entirely from the user without changing the backend.

Monitoring lag

Every serious database exposes lag metrics:

  • MySQL: Seconds_Behind_Master — but it is unreliable, especially for multi-source replication. Prefer comparing GTIDs.
  • PostgreSQL: the difference between the primary's WAL LSN and the replica's pg_last_wal_replay_lsn(), expressed in bytes or seconds.
  • Generic: a heartbeat table where the leader writes a timestamp and the follower reads it — the difference is the lag. This is portable and unambiguous.

Lag should be alarmed on, because an unnoticed growing lag is either a precursor to data loss (the follower will be promoted and be missing data) or a sign that the system is about to become unavailable.

When lag becomes unbounded

Sometimes the lag does not recover — a follower is too far behind, or a burst overwhelms it permanently. The mitigations: remove the follower from rotation, take a fresh snapshot and rebuild it, add capacity to the follower, or (in the worst case) promote a different follower and rebuild. A design that reads from any follower without checking its lag is a design that will serve increasingly stale data under stress.

Common Pitfall

Load-balancing reads across replicas without considering session stickiness. A user's reads can be served by different replicas with different lag, causing the UI to flicker between stale and fresh values. The fix is session-affinity for reads, or monotonic-read guarantees via a version token.

7

What is split-brain, and how do you prevent it during failover?

Short Answer

Split-brain is when two nodes each believe they are the leader — usually because a network partition separated them and each lost contact with the other, so each promoted itself or failed to notice it was demoted. Both then accept writes, which diverge and cannot be merged. Prevention requires two things: a quorum-based leader election, and fencing — a mechanism that makes the old leader unable to write once it has been superseded.

How split-brain happens

A three-node cluster: leader A, followers B and C. The network partitions A from B and C. B and C decide A is dead and elect B as the new leader. But A is not actually dead — it is just isolated. A still thinks it is the leader and continues to accept writes from any client that can still reach it. Now A and B are both accepting writes, and their data diverges.

This is not rare. It happens with datacenter connectivity issues, a network interface failing, a GC pause long enough to trigger a heartbeat timeout, or an operator error. It is the single most damaging failure mode in leader-based replication, because the data divergence may be unrecoverable.

Prevention 1 — Quorum-based election

The first defence is that a new leader can only be elected with a majority of the cluster. In a three-node cluster, a new leader requires two votes; A alone cannot elect itself, and B + C together form a majority. Raft, Paxos, and ZAB all enforce this: a leader must be elected by a quorum, and a node that cannot reach a quorum cannot become leader. This ensures that at most one leader can be elected for a given term.

The corollary is that a cluster that loses quorum (more than half its nodes) cannot elect a leader and cannot accept writes. This is deliberate: it is better to be unavailable than to be split-brained. A two-node cluster is therefore always a bad idea — losing either node makes the cluster unavailable, and the split-brain risk is higher because there is no majority to arbitrate.

Prevention 2 — Fencing tokens

Quorum election ensures at most one leader is elected, but it does not prevent the old leader from continuing to accept writes if it does not know it has been demoted. It might be isolated, alive, and happily serving stale clients.

The fix is a fencing token: each leadership term gets a monotonically increasing number, and every write carries the token. Storage — the actual disk or database — rejects writes with a lower token than the highest it has seen. So even if the old leader thinks it is in charge, its writes are rejected by the storage layer once the new leader has written with a higher token. The old leader eventually learns it is stale and steps down.

Fencing requires that the storage layer (or every shared resource the leader touches) checks the token. This is not automatic — it must be designed in. But it is the definitive prevention against split-brain damage, and it is used in every serious consensus system.

Prevention 3 — STONITH and node isolation

An older approach: when a leader is suspected of being partition, physically kill it — "shoot the other node in the head". This is STONITH (Shoot The Other Node In The Head), a hardware fencing technique that powers off or network-isolates the suspect node before promoting a new one. Crude but effective, and used in many enterprise HA clusters where the failure modes are well understood.

Detection is the hard part

All of these mechanisms depend on correctly detecting that the leader is dead or unreachable. The fundamental difficulty is that a slow node and a dead node look identical from the outside — both fail to respond within a timeout. Set the timeout too short and you get false failovers (a busy but healthy leader is demoted); set it too long and you have extended downtime. The standard mitigation is a longer timeout than any reasonable GC pause, plus a lease (the leader's term expires after a fixed wall-clock interval unless renewed) so that even a stalled leader eventually stops acting as leader.

What split-brain costs

Data divergence, which must be resolved manually if it occurs. In a payment system, two sets of transactions that each think they are canonical. In an inventory system, two counts. The resolution is painful and often involves choosing one side's data and discarding the other. The engineering effort of prevention is trivially small compared to the cost of remediation.

Interview Framing

Say: “I'll prevent split-brain with quorum election — a leader needs a majority — and fencing tokens so the old leader's writes are rejected even if it's still alive. And I'll use an odd number of nodes so a single failure can't destroy quorum.” That covers election, fencing, and topology in three sentences.

8

How do you handle conflict resolution with LWW, vector clocks, and CRDTs?

Short Answer

Conflicts arise whenever multiple replicas can accept writes without coordination — multi-leader, leaderless, and offline-first. There are four strategies: last-writer-wins (simple, loses data), version vectors (detect concurrency, keep both values for the application to resolve), CRDTs (merge automatically, limited data types), and application-defined merge (correct for the domain, requires domain logic). The right choice depends on whether the conflict can be resolved automatically and whether losing a write is acceptable.

The four strategies

StrategyMechanismProsCons
Last-writer-wins (LWW) Highest timestamp wins Trivial; automatic; no metadata beyond a clock Silently loses one write; depends on clock sync; wrong when clocks skew
Version vectors Track per-replica versions; detect if writes are concurrent or ordered Detects conflicts accurately; keeps both values for later resolution Metadata grows with replica count; the application must resolve
CRDTs Commutative merge — mathematically guaranteed convergence Automatic and correct for the supported types Only certain data structures; metadata overhead; cannot enforce arbitrary invariants
Application-defined merge Domain logic decides — e.g., union shopping carts, sum counters Correct for the business domain Requires domain expertise; must be written for each type

Last-writer-wins in depth

LWW attaches a timestamp to each write; the replica with the higher timestamp survives. It is used by Cassandra (default), DynamoDB Global Tables, and most "conflict-free" multi-region databases. The advantage is that it never fails to produce a value — no conflict handling, no application changes. The disadvantages are severe:

  • Silent data loss. One user's write is discarded with no notification. They see their update succeed, then discover it was overwritten.
  • Clock skew. Two nodes with clocks off by seconds will produce incorrect ordering. A write from the faster clock always wins, even if the other write was objectively later. This is a real production failure mode with NTP drift.
  • No causal awareness. LWW can "resurrect" a deleted item if an earlier write has a later timestamp than the delete.

LWW is acceptable for counters (approximately), presence, session heartbeats, and low-stakes preferences. It is not acceptable for user content, financial data, or anything where the losing write matters.

Version vectors in depth

A version vector tracks, for each replica, how many writes it has applied. When replica A receives a write from replica B, it compares B's version vector with its own:

  • If B's is strictly greater, B's write is newer — apply it.
  • If A's is strictly greater, A's is newer — discard B's.
  • If neither dominates, the writes are concurrent — a genuine conflict.

Riak uses this approach, returning multiple "siblings" to the client so the application can merge. The client might union shopping carts, sum counters, or ask the user. The trade-off is that the metadata grows with the number of replicas (O(N) per key), and the application must handle the multi-value case.

CRDTs in depth

Conflict-free Replicated Data Types are covered in Volume 3, Question 12. Their property is that the merge is commutative, associative, and idempotent, so any order of delivery converges. They are the right answer for counters, sets, sequences, and collaborative editing. They are the wrong answer for anything with a global invariant — you cannot enforce "balance ≥ 0" with a CRDT because two replicas can both approve a debit independently.

Application-defined merge

Often the correct answer is to define merge logic for each data type:

  • Set-like data (tags, likes): union of adds, union of removes — an OR-Set or a simple set union.
  • Counters: sum of per-replica deltas — a PN-Counter.
  • Text editing: a sequence CRDT or operational transformation.
  • Numeric totals in accounting: append-only ledger entries — never overwrite; always append a correcting entry.

The honest guidance

The first question is not "which conflict resolution?" but "must this data be writable in multiple places?" For most financial, inventory, and identity data, the answer is no — shard by user or entity so that one node owns the record, and conflicts cannot occur. For the smaller set of genuinely multi-writer data (carts, collaborative documents, presence, feeds), pick a conflict strategy and document it. LWW is a decision to lose data silently; that must be a conscious, product-level decision.

Common Pitfall

Using last-write-wins and assuming it "just works". It works silently and lossily. If a user complains "my edit disappeared", the answer is not a bug — it is the chosen policy. Teams should either accept that explicitly or choose a strategy that preserves both writes.

What are anti-entropy, Merkle trees, and read repair?

Short Answer

These are the three mechanisms that bring divergent replicas back into agreement. Read repair fixes a discrepancy discovered during a read. Anti-entropy is a background process that compares replicas and fixes differences even for keys that are never read. Merkle trees are hash trees that make anti-entropy efficient — comparing two replicas requires exchanging only a handful of hashes rather than comparing every key.

Read repair

When a client reads a key from R replicas and discovers that they disagree, the coordinator writes the latest version back to the stale replicas. The next read from those replicas returns the corrected value. This is cheap — it piggybacks on the read path — and it keeps frequently-read keys fresh. Its limitation is obvious: it only repairs keys that are actually read. A key that is written once and read once a year will remain inconsistent for up to a year.

Read repair can be synchronous (block the read until repair completes) or asynchronous (return the fresh value now, repair in the background). Synchronous is slower but the read is guaranteed to have propagated the fix; asynchronous is faster but the repair may fail silently. Cassandra uses both modes depending on the consistency level.

Anti-entropy

A background process that periodically compares every replica's data with every other's and repairs the differences. It catches everything read repair missed — keys that were never read. It is the safety net that guarantees eventual convergence.

The naive implementation compares every key: O(K) time and bandwidth. For a dataset of a billion keys, this is infeasible. This is where Merkle trees come in.

Merkle trees

A Merkle tree is a hash tree over a key space. The leaves are hashes of individual keys (or small ranges of keys); each internal node is the hash of its children. The root hash summarises the entire tree.

LevelContent
Leaveshash(key, value) for each key, or hash of a range bucket
Level 1hash(leaf1 + leaf2) for each pair
Level 2hash(level1 pair) ...
Roothash of everything

Two replicas exchange root hashes. If they match, they are identical — done. If they differ, they descend into the child hashes and compare. At each level, only the subtrees whose hashes differ need further exploration. If only 10 keys differ out of a billion, the comparison finds them in O(log K) exchanges rather than O(K). This is the same structure Git uses for its object store and Bitcoin for block integrity.

Cassandra, Dynamo, and Riak all use Merkle trees for anti-entropy. The tree is rebuilt periodically (or incrementally maintained), and the diff is a handful of hash exchanges.

The repair pipeline in practice

  1. Write path: write reaches W replicas; the rest catch up later via hinted handoff.
  2. Read path: reads query R replicas; if they disagree, read repair fixes the stale ones.
  3. Hinted handoff: failed replica's writes are held by a peer and delivered on recovery (Question 10).
  4. Anti-entropy: periodic Merkle-tree comparison catches everything the above missed.

These are layered defences, each catching what the previous one missed. Read repair is fast and reactive; hinted handoff handles transient failures; anti-entropy guarantees eventual convergence for all keys.

Costs and tuning

Anti-entropy is expensive — it consumes network bandwidth and CPU on both replicas. It is usually run during off-peak hours with rate limiting. The interval (e.g., nightly) is a trade-off between convergence time and cost. Merkle trees must be rebuilt when data changes, which is itself expensive; some systems maintain them incrementally, others rebuild on a schedule.

Trade-off

Read repair is cheap but only fixes what is read. Anti-entropy fixes everything but is expensive. The standard design is both: read repair for the hot path, anti-entropy for the long tail. The Merkle tree is what makes the latter affordable.

What are hinted handoff and sloppy quorums?

Short Answer

Sloppy quorum allows a write to be acknowledged by nodes that are not the designated replicas for the key — any live nodes will do. Hinted handoff is the mechanism that makes it work: the temporary node stores a "hint" that the write belongs to a specific replica, and delivers it once that replica recovers. Together they preserve write availability during a failure, at the cost of weakening the strict quorum overlap guarantee.

The problem

In a leaderless system with N=3 and W=2, a write must reach two of the three designated replicas. If one of them is down, the writer can reach only two — but if a different node has also failed, it might reach only one. Strictly, the write must fail. Sloppy quorum says: rather than fail, write to any available node, and the system remains available.

How sloppy quorum works

A client sends the write to the N designated replicas. If some are unreachable, the coordinator walks the consistent-hash ring and sends the write to the next available nodes instead. It counts those as acknowledgements. With W acknowledgements (from designated or substitute nodes), the write is considered successful. The client does not know or care that some replicas were substitutes.

How hinted handoff completes it

The substitute node cannot hold the data forever — it is not a designated replica, and other clients will read from the correct nodes. So it stores the write as a hint: a message that says "this write belongs to replica R, deliver when R comes back." A background process periodically checks whether R is reachable, and if so, delivers the hint and deletes its local copy.

If R never comes back (hardware failure), the hint is held until R is replaced. If R stays down long enough, the hint may expire and the write is lost from that replica — but it is still on the other W replicas, so the data is not lost as long as the write itself succeeded. The hint is a courtesy, not a durability guarantee.

The consistency cost

This is where the subtlety lives. The standard quorum argument is that R + W > N guarantees overlap among the designated replicas. With sloppy quorum, some of the W acknowledgements may come from substitute nodes, which means the actual designated-replica set has fewer than W copies. A subsequent read quorum of R designated replicas may not overlap with those substitutes — so it may miss the write entirely.

Concretely: N=3, W=2. Replica A is down; the write goes to A' (a substitute) and B. That is two acknowledgements — the write succeeds. Now a read with R=2 queries A and C. A does not have the write (it was never delivered), C does not have it, and A' (which does) is not queried. The read returns stale data despite R + W > N.

This is exactly the scenario that makes sloppy quorums and R + W > N not equivalent to strong consistency. Dynamo's original paper was explicit about this: sloppy quorum favours availability, and the consistency is "eventual" rather than "guaranteed at read time".

When hinted handoff is appropriate

  • Transient failures. A node is temporarily unreachable, expected to return within minutes or hours. Hint delivery is fast and the system does not lose availability.
  • High write availability requirement. The business genuinely prefers a write to succeed over strict consistency. Feeds, carts, activity streams.
  • Combined with read repair and anti-entropy. The hint is one mechanism among several; the others catch any gap it leaves.

When it is a mistake

  • Anything requiring strong consistency. Financial transactions, inventory, ledgers. The sloppy quorum breaks the read-time guarantee.
  • Long node outages. If a node is down for days, hint storage accumulates on substitutes, which is itself a problem, and the eventual catch-up is large.
  • Strict durability requirements. A write acknowledged by a substitute node is not on the canonical replicas; if the substitute also fails, the hint is lost.
Common Pitfall

Assuming a system with sloppy quorums provides the same consistency guarantee as one with strict quorums. It does not. The documentation usually says "eventually consistent"; the operational reality is that reads can return stale data even when R + W > N. Choose strict quorums for consistency-critical data, and sloppy quorums for availability-critical data — and do not mix them within one dataset.

What is the difference between physical and logical replication?

Short Answer

Physical replication ships the exact bytes of storage changes — WAL records, data pages — producing byte-identical replicas that must run the same engine version and schema. Logical replication ships the semantic changes — "insert this row, update this column" — which can be applied to a different schema, engine version, or even a different database. Physical is for HA and DR; logical is for migration, fan-out, and change data capture.

AspectPhysicalLogical
What is shippedWAL records, data blocksRow-level inserts/updates/deletes
Replica fidelityByte-identical to primaryLogically equivalent, can differ physically
Version compatibilityMust match engine version exactlyCan differ — even different engines
Schema flexibilitySame schema, same indexesCan be subset, different schema, different indexes
SelectivityWhole database (or tablespace)Selected tables, columns, rows
Overhead on primaryLow — just ship the WALHigher — must decode and publish
Typical useHA standby, disaster recoveryMigration, CDC, multi-database fan-out
ExamplesPostgreSQL streaming replication, MySQL binlog (physical-ish)PostgreSQL logical decoding, MySQL ROW binlog, Debezium

Physical replication in detail

The primary writes to its write-ahead log. A standby streams the WAL, applies it, and becomes byte-identical. Because it is byte-identical, it can be promoted instantly to primary (no reconstruction needed) — which is why physical replication is the standard for HA. The constraint is rigidity: a PostgreSQL 15 standby cannot replicate from a PostgreSQL 16 primary, and you cannot replicate only a subset of tables.

Logical replication in detail

The primary decodes the WAL into logical change events — the actual rows that changed, with before/after values. These can be published to subscribers, transformed, or sent to external consumers. The replica applies the same row-level changes into its own tables, which may have different indexes, different schema, or be a different database entirely.

The trade-offs: logical replication requires more CPU on the primary (decoding) and on the replica (re-applying). It also requires the primary to retain WAL long enough for slow consumers, which is a disk-usage concern. It does not replicate DDL in many engines (PostgreSQL historically required schema changes to be done manually on both sides). But it is enormously more flexible.

What each is good for

  • Physical: HA standby that can be promoted in seconds, disaster recovery, read replicas of the exact same data. This is the default for a single-database deployment.
  • Logical: migrating between major versions (zero-downtime upgrade via logical replication to a new-version replica), migrating between clouds or engines, replicating a subset of tables, feeding data to a warehouse or search index, and CDC (Question 12).

MySQL's slightly different model

MySQL's binary log can operate in statement, row, or mixed mode. Row-based binlog is effectively logical — it records the actual row changes. Statement-based is effectively logical too, but with the pitfalls of non-deterministic statements. MySQL does not have a separate "physical replication" mode in the PostgreSQL sense; the binary log is a logical stream. This is why MySQL replication is more forgiving of version differences than PostgreSQL streaming.

Choosing between them

Use physical when: you need HA/DR, the replicas must be identical, and the primary is the same engine version. Use logical when: you need to transform the data, replicate a subset, migrate between versions or engines, or feed downstream systems. Many production systems use both — physical for the HA standby, logical for CDC to the warehouse and search index.

Interview Framing

Say: “I'll use physical replication for the hot standby that can be promoted, and logical replication to feed the search index and the data warehouse.” That demonstrates you understand the two roles are different, not competing.

What is change data capture (CDC), and how does it relate to replication?

Short Answer

CDC is the process of capturing row-level changes from a database's log and publishing them as a stream of events to downstream consumers — search indexes, caches, data warehouses, other services. It is built on logical replication: the database's own write-ahead log is the source of truth, and CDC turns it into a change stream without polling and without duplicating writes.

Why CDC exists

The naive alternative is dual-write: after writing to the database, the application also publishes an event to a message queue. This is straightforward and catastrophically wrong:

  • Atomicity failure. The DB write succeeds and the event publish fails (or vice versa). No transaction covers both.
  • Ordering failure. Two writes in quick succession may publish events out of order.
  • Missed writes. A batch job, a migration, or a direct SQL update bypasses the application and never publishes an event.
  • Duplicate writes. The application writes, retries, writes again — two events for one logical change.

CDC eliminates all of these by using the database log as the single source of change. The log is ordered, complete, and already transactional. If a transaction commits, every change is in the log; if it rolls back, none are. CDC reads the log and publishes in order.

How it works

  1. Enable logical replication (or use the database's native CDC mechanism) on the source.
  2. A CDC connector — Debezium is the standard, alongside native connectors in cloud databases — reads the log and decodes changes into structured events.
  3. The events are published to a message broker (Kafka, Pub/Sub) or directly to consumers.
  4. Consumers apply the changes to their own stores: Elasticsearch for search, Redis for cache, ClickHouse or BigQuery for analytics, another database for a service.

Exactly-once and ordering

CDC streams are almost always at-least-once — a consumer may receive the same event twice after a retry or rebalance. Consumers must be idempotent: applying the same insert twice should not create two records, applying the same update twice should produce the same result. This usually means including the primary key and a version/timestamp in the event, and using upsert semantics on the consumer side.

Ordering is per-key, not global. Kafka preserves order within a partition; partitioning by the row's primary key ensures that changes to the same row arrive in order, which is what consumers actually need. Cross-row ordering is not guaranteed and is not usually required.

Schema evolution

The source schema changes — a column is added, renamed, dropped. The CDC stream must communicate this to consumers. The standard approach is a schema registry (Confluent Schema Registry is the common one) that holds versioned Avro/Protobuf schemas, with compatibility rules (backward, forward, full). Consumers can evolve at their own pace as long as the schemas remain compatible. Without this, a schema change silently breaks every downstream consumer.

CDC vs replication vs polling

MechanismLatencyLoad on sourceCompleteness
CDC (log-based)MillisecondsLow — reads the logAll changes, including batch and direct SQL
Polling (WHERE updated_at > ?)Polling interval (seconds to minutes)High — repeated queriesMisses deletes, misses changes without updated_at
Dual-write from applicationImmediateNone on DBMisses any non-application write

What CDC is used for

  • Search index synchronisation. Keep Elasticsearch in sync with the operational database — the canonical use case.
  • Cache invalidation. Publish change events that invalidate cached entries.
  • Data warehouse ingestion. Stream operational changes into an analytics store with seconds of latency, replacing nightly batch ETL.
  • Microservice data sync. Propagate a service's changes to other services that need a read model — CQRS-style.
  • Audit logs. Every change becomes an event, giving a complete history.

The operational concerns

CDC introduces a new component (the connector) that must be monitored, and a new failure mode: a stalled connector means the downstream store falls behind. The connector's position in the log (the LSN or offset) is the key metric — if it is not advancing, something is wrong. Also, the source database must retain WAL long enough for the connector to keep up; a slow connector can cause the source to accumulate log, filling disk.

Trade-off

CDC eliminates dual-write inconsistency and gives a complete, ordered change stream, at the cost of an extra connector, a schema registry, and a new class of operational concerns. For any system with a search index, a cache, or a warehouse that must stay fresh, it is worth the cost. For a small system with one database and no downstream, it is over-engineering.

How do you design a multi-region replication strategy?

Short Answer

There is no single answer — it is a family of strategies along the consistency/latency/cost spectrum. The three dominant patterns are active-passive (one region writes, the other is standby), regional ownership (users are pinned to a home region, cross-region replication is async for DR and cross-region reads), and active-active (every region writes, conflicts are resolved with LWW or CRDTs). The choice is driven by RPO, RTO, latency SLO, and whether the data tolerates conflicts.

The three patterns

PatternConsistencyWrite latencyRPOUse case
Active-passive Strong in active region Local (~ms) Seconds (async) to zero (sync) Cost-sensitive; failover on regional outage; compliance
Regional ownership (sharded by user/geo) Strong within region Local (~ms) Seconds cross-region Most SaaS — user data lives where the user does
Active-active Eventual with conflict resolution Local (~ms) Zero within sync window, conflicts resolved later Global UGC, carts, feeds, presence
Global consensus Strong globally (linearizable) Cross-region (~100 ms) Zero Financial ledgers, inventory, identity

Active-passive in detail

One region is the active primary and handles all reads and writes. A second region runs a replica (or a full standby cluster) that receives changes asynchronously. On failure of the primary region, traffic is switched to the standby — either via DNS failover, a global load balancer, or manual intervention. This is the simplest to reason about and the cheapest to run (the standby is idle or read-only). The RPO depends on replication mode: sync gives zero loss but adds latency; async gives a window of potential loss.

The failure mode is the failover itself: DNS TTLs mean the switch takes minutes, the standby may be behind, and the old primary's un-replicated writes are lost. Testing failover regularly is mandatory, because the first time you discover the runbook is wrong should not be during an actual outage.

Regional ownership — the pragmatic default

Users are assigned a home region (by geography, by tenant, by hash). Their data lives in that region and is read/written there with low latency and strong consistency. The data is replicated asynchronously to other regions for disaster recovery and cross-region reads, but those reads are eventually consistent. A user traveling to another region gets a slightly stale view, which is usually acceptable.

This pattern is what most large SaaS systems actually implement. It gives the latency benefit of being "local" to the user, the consistency benefit of a single writer per record (no conflicts), and the DR benefit of cross-region replication — all without the complexity of multi-region writes. Its constraint is that a user cannot write to two regions simultaneously; if they could, the home region is still the authority for their data.

Active-active in detail

Every region accepts writes and replicates asynchronously to the others. This gives the lowest write latency (always local) and highest availability (any region can serve even if others are down), but it makes conflicts inevitable. The system must have a conflict resolution policy — LWW, CRDTs, or application-specific merge — and must accept that concurrent writes to the same record lose data or require reconciliation. Active-active is appropriate for data where that is acceptable (feeds, carts, presence, counters) and wrong for data where it is not (ledgers, inventory, identity).

Global consensus

Spanner, CockroachDB, and YugabyteDB provide linearizable reads and writes across regions using a replicated consensus protocol (Raft or Paxos). This gives zero RPO and strong consistency but at a latency cost — every write pays a cross-region round trip, typically 100+ ms. It is the right choice for data where the business cannot tolerate any loss or conflict, and the latency is acceptable. It is expensive and operationally complex, which is why it is reserved for the few datasets that genuinely require it.

Choosing between them

Ask four questions:

  1. What is the RPO? Zero means sync replication or consensus. Seconds is acceptable for async.
  2. What is the RTO? Minutes means active-passive with automatic failover. Seconds means active-active or regional ownership.
  3. Does the data tolerate conflicts? If yes, active-active is available. If no, regional ownership or consensus.
  4. What is the write latency budget? Under 10 ms means local writes (regional or active-active). Over 100 ms allows consensus.

Data residency and compliance

A critical non-technical constraint: GDPR, data sovereignty, and industry regulations often require that certain data never leaves a region. This pushes towards regional ownership with strict isolation — the European data lives in Europe and is never replicated outside. Designing for residency from the start is far easier than retrofitting it: the tenant or user record needs a region field, and every query must respect it.

Interview Framing

Say: “I'll shard users by region so writes are local and strongly consistent, replicate asynchronously for DR with an RPO of a few seconds, and keep the ledger on a global-consensus store because the RPO there must be zero.” That names three patterns for three data types and shows you understand that a single strategy is rarely correct.
