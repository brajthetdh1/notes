Part A — Kafka Architecture & Data Model Brokers, partitions, replicas, and the controller

1

Design a pub/sub system like Kafka that supports topics, partitions, producers, consumers, and consumer groups.

Short Answer

A Kafka-like pub/sub system is a partitioned, replicated, append-only log. A topic is split into partitions; each partition is an ordered log with one leader and N-1 followers; producers append to partitions; consumers in a group each own a subset of partitions and track their offset; a controller manages cluster metadata and leader election. The design's power comes from the log abstraction: everything else — replay, fan-out, ordering, exactly-once — follows from it.

The abstraction: a log, not a queue

The fundamental design choice is that messages are appended to a log and retained, not deleted on consumption. A consumer reads from the log at its own offset; advancing the offset is the consumer's business, not the broker's. Multiple consumer groups read the same log independently, each at its own offset. This single choice gives replay, multiple independent consumers, and decoupling between producer and consumer pace — none of which a destructive queue can provide.

Topics and partitions

A topic is a named stream of messages. It is divided into partitions, which are the unit of parallelism, replication, and ordering. Each partition is an independent, ordered log. Messages within a partition have a strict order defined by their offset; messages across partitions have no ordering guarantee. The producer chooses a partition via a key hash (same key → same partition → same order) or round-robin (no ordering, better balance).

Partitioning is what makes the system scale: 100 partitions can be spread across 10 brokers, each broker handling 10 partitions, and each partition's throughput adds to the total. It also bounds the consumer parallelism: a consumer group can have at most one consumer per partition, so the maximum parallelism is the partition count.

Brokers and replication

A broker is a server that stores partitions and serves producers and consumers. Each partition has one leader (which accepts writes and serves reads for consistency-sensitive consumers) and N-1 followers (which replicate from the leader). The replication factor is typically 3. Followers that are caught up to the leader's high-water mark form the ISR (in-sync replica set). If the leader fails, a follower from the ISR is promoted; if all ISR members fail, the partition is unavailable unless unclean leader election is enabled (which risks data loss).

The controller

One broker is elected controller (via KRaft or ZooKeeper). The controller maintains cluster metadata: which brokers are alive, which partitions they own, which replica is leader, which replicas are in the ISR. It detects broker failures via heartbeats and triggers leader election. The controller is a metadata-only role — it does not carry message traffic — but it is critical to cluster health, and its availability is a prerequisite for partitions being available.

Producers

A producer serializes a message, chooses a partition (by key hash or round-robin), batches messages for the same partition, compresses the batch, and sends it to the partition leader. The leader appends to its log and replicates; the producer receives an ack based on its acks setting. The producer also handles retries, metadata refresh on leader changes, and idempotence (sequence numbers per partition) to avoid duplicates from retries.

Consumers and consumer groups

A consumer subscribes to a topic and joins a consumer group. The group coordinator assigns each partition to exactly one consumer in the group, so the group collectively consumes the topic once, with the work distributed. Each consumer tracks its offset locally, and periodically commits it to the broker (in the __consumer_offsets topic) so a restart or rebalance resumes from the committed position. A rebalance reassigns partitions when consumers join or leave.

The read path

Consumers fetch from the leader (or from a follower if fetch-from-follower is configured for cross-datacenter reads) by offset. The broker serves reads from the page cache for recently written segments, and from disk for older ones, with a sparse offset index to seek efficiently. The consumer reads a batch, processes it, and commits the next offset. This is why Kafka can sustain high read throughput on commodity hardware: reads are sequential and mostly served from memory.

What the design enables

  • Fan-out: any number of consumer groups can read the same topic, each at its own offset.
  • Replay: a consumer can seek back and reprocess, as long as the messages are retained.
  • Ordering: per-partition, which is sufficient for most use cases (per-user, per-order).
  • Throughput: partitioning and sequential I/O scale horizontally.
  • Durability: replication with acks-all and ISR-based leader election ensures no committed message is lost.
Trade-off

The log model gives replay and fan-out, but it costs storage (messages are retained, not deleted), and it constrains ordering to partitions (no global order). A destructive queue is simpler and cheaper for small volumes, but cannot replay and cannot serve multiple independent consumers without duplication. Kafka's model is the right one when fan-out, replay, or scale matter; the destructive queue is right when none do.

Interview Framing

Open with: “This is a partitioned log, not a queue. Topics split into partitions; each partition is a replicated ordered log; consumers read at their own offset; the controller manages leadership.” That names the abstraction and its consequences before any component diagram.

2

Explain Kafka's architecture from the ground up: brokers, topics, partitions, replicas, and the role of ZooKeeper (or KRaft).

Short Answer

A Kafka cluster is a set of brokers (servers) that store partitions. A topic is a logical stream split into partitions; each partition is replicated across brokers with one leader and followers. A controller (elected via ZooKeeper historically, via KRaft in modern versions) manages metadata: broker liveness, partition assignment, and leader election. Producers write to partition leaders; consumers read from them and track offsets.

Brokers

A broker is a Kafka server. It stores the partitions assigned to it (each as a set of log segment files on disk), serves producer and consumer requests, replicates partitions from leader to follower, and participates in controller election. A cluster typically has 3–30 brokers; more brokers means more partitions can be spread and more parallel I/O. Brokers are identified by an integer ID, and partition placement is determined by the controller.

Topics and partitions

A topic is a named stream, created with a partition count and replication factor. Each partition is an ordered, immutable log with a monotonically increasing offset. Partitions are the unit of parallelism and ordering. The partition count is fixed at creation (it can be increased, but not decreased, and increasing it changes key-to-partition mapping), so it must be chosen with future scale in mind.

Replicas and leaders

Each partition has a replication factor R (typically 3). One replica is the leader; the others are followers. Producers write to the leader; followers fetch from the leader and append to their own logs. Consumers read from the leader (by default) or from a follower (for cross-datacenter reads). The ISR (in-sync replica set) is the subset of replicas that are caught up to the leader's high-water mark; only ISR members are eligible for leader election.

The controller

The controller is one broker elected by the cluster. It maintains the cluster metadata: broker liveness, partition leadership, ISR membership, topic configuration. It detects broker failures via heartbeats and reassigns leadership. It is a metadata role — it does not carry message traffic — but it is critical to cluster availability, and a controller failure triggers a new election (which takes seconds and briefly delays metadata changes).

KRaft replaced ZooKeeper by embedding a Raft-based metadata quorum in Kafka itself. The controller quorum is a set of controller nodes (typically 3 or 5) that replicate metadata via Raft; one is the active controller, the others are hot standbys. This removes the operational burden of ZooKeeper, removes the ZooKeeper write bottleneck that limited clusters to ~200k partitions, and makes failover faster and more reliable.

The write path in full

  1. Producer sends a batch to a partition leader (after metadata lookup).
  2. Leader appends to its log, assigns offsets, and updates the high-water mark (the highest offset replicated to all ISR members).
  3. Followers fetch from the leader, append, and send their high-water mark.
  4. Leader acks the producer based on acks: 0 (no ack), 1 (leader only), all (all ISR).
  5. The message becomes visible to consumers once it is at or below the high-water mark (for read-committed consumers) or immediately (for read-uncommitted consumers).

The read path

Consumers fetch by offset from the leader. The broker reads from the page cache if the segment is recent, or from disk with an offset index to seek. The consumer processes the batch, commits the next offset to the coordinator, and repeats. Fetch size and max poll records control batching; the consumer's throughput is bounded by the fetch size times the poll frequency.

What the architecture optimizes for

Sequential I/O (appends are the fastest disk pattern), horizontal scaling (partitions spread across brokers), replication for durability, and decoupling of producer and consumer pace (the log absorbs bursts and lets consumers catch up later). The architecture does not optimize for per-message routing (no exchanges), per-message priority (no priority queues), or global ordering (only per-partition). Those are the costs of the log model, and they are acceptable for the workloads Kafka targets.

Common Pitfall

Assuming the controller is on the data path. It is not — producers and consumers talk to partition leaders directly. A controller failure delays metadata changes (leader election, topic creation) but does not stop message flow on existing partitions. Confusing the two leads to over-estimating the controller's criticality.

3

What is a Kafka partition, and why does it matter? How does partitioning relate to ordering and parallelism?

Short Answer

A partition is an ordered, immutable log that is the unit of parallelism, replication, and ordering in Kafka. Messages within a partition are strictly ordered by offset; messages across partitions are not. More partitions means more parallel producers, consumers, and brokers — but also more overhead, longer rebalances, and no cross-partition ordering.

The partition as the atomic unit

Kafka's design decisions all reduce to the partition. A topic is a collection of partitions; a broker hosts some partitions; a leader is a per-partition role; a consumer group assigns partitions to consumers. There is no cluster-wide ordering or atomicity — every guarantee and every scaling property is scoped to a partition. Understanding this is the key to understanding Kafka's trade-offs.

Ordering

Within a partition, messages are appended in order, each with a monotonically increasing offset. A consumer reading from the partition receives them in that order. This is the only ordering guarantee Kafka provides, and it is sufficient for most use cases because the partition key usually matches the entity whose order matters: per-user events, per-order events, per-device telemetry. All events for a given key land on the same partition (because the producer hashes the key), so their order is preserved.

Across partitions, there is no order. A consumer reading all partitions of a topic may see events from different partitions interleaved arbitrarily. If the application needs global order, it must either use a single partition (which sacrifices parallelism) or impose order at read time (sorting, sequence numbers, timestamps) — which is expensive and usually a sign that the ordering requirement should be scoped to a key.

Parallelism

Partitions are the unit of producer and consumer parallelism. A producer with 100 partitions can write to 100 partitions in parallel, using multiple threads and multiple broker connections. A consumer group with 100 partitions can have up to 100 consumer instances, each processing one partition. Adding partitions increases the maximum throughput and the maximum consumer parallelism, at the cost of more per-partition overhead.

The consumer parallelism is capped at the partition count — extra consumers sit idle. This is why partition count is chosen for the maximum expected consumer parallelism, not just the current producer throughput. A topic with 10 partitions can never have more than 10 active consumers in a group, no matter how much lag accumulates.

The key-to-partition mapping

The producer chooses a partition via a partitioner. The default is a hash of the key mod the partition count, which gives the same key the same partition (order preserved) and distributes keys across partitions (balance, assuming keys are uniformly distributed). Without a key, the producer round-robins or uses a sticky partitioner that batches consecutive messages to the same partition for efficiency.

The key choice is therefore a design decision: if the key is the user ID, per-user order is preserved and per-user throughput is bounded by one partition. If the key is a finer-grained ID, more parallelism but no per-user order. If there is no key, maximum parallelism but no ordering at all. The key must be chosen to match the ordering requirement.

Replication and failure

Each partition is replicated independently. If a broker fails, only the partitions it led are affected; the others continue serving. The replication unit is the partition, so replication cost scales with partition count: more partitions means more replicas to fetch, more ISR tracking, and more leader elections when a broker fails. A broker with 1,000 partition replicas that fails triggers 1,000 leader elections, which can take seconds to minutes.

Hot partitions

A partition can be hot (receiving disproportionate traffic) if the key distribution is skewed. A celebrity user ID, a best-selling product ID, or a large tenant ID creates a hot partition, which becomes a bottleneck because its leader broker is overloaded while others idle. The fixes are key salting (prefix the key with a random or hashed suffix to spread it), a different key, or dedicated partitions for known hot keys. The partition model does not prevent hotspots; it concentrates them.

Changing partition count

Partitions can be added but not removed. Adding partitions changes the key-to-partition mapping for all keys (because the hash mod N changes), which breaks ordering for keys that move — the same key now lands on a different partition, and messages before and after the change can be out of order relative to each other. The standard workaround is to over-provision at creation or to use a partitioner that preserves ordering across changes (rare). This is why partition count is a decision made once and lived with.

Interview Framing

Say: “Partitions are the unit of ordering and parallelism. I'll choose the partition key to match the entity whose order matters — user_id for per-user event streams — and size the partition count for the maximum consumer parallelism I expect.” That names both constraints and the decision they drive.

4

How do you choose the right number of partitions for a topic? What are the trade-offs of too few vs too many?

Short Answer

Size partitions for the maximum consumer parallelism you expect, not the current throughput. A practical starting point is max(producer throughput / per-partition throughput, consumer parallelism), then round up and over-provision by 1.5–2×. Too few caps throughput and cannot scale consumers; too many increases rebalance time, broker overhead, and end-to-end latency.

The two constraints

Partition count is bounded from below by throughput and from above by overhead. The lower bound comes from the per-partition throughput ceiling: a single partition leader can sustain roughly 10–50 MB/s on commodity hardware, so a topic writing 200 MB/s needs at least 4–20 partitions. The other lower bound is consumer parallelism: a group cannot have more consumers than partitions, so a topic that must support 50 parallel consumers needs at least 50 partitions.

The upper bound comes from the cost of each partition. Every partition is an open file handle, a replication stream, an ISR entry, and a piece of controller metadata. Thousands of partitions on a cluster are manageable; hundreds of thousands strain the controller, increase rebalance time, and make leader elections slow. KRaft raises the ceiling substantially (millions of partitions in theory), but the per-partition cost does not disappear.

A practical sizing method

  1. Estimate target throughput (e.g., 100 MB/s peak).
  2. Divide by per-partition throughput (e.g., 20 MB/s) → 5 partitions minimum for writes.
  3. Estimate consumer parallelism (e.g., 20 consumers to keep up) → 20 partitions minimum.
  4. Take the maximum → 20 partitions.
  5. Over-provision 1.5–2× for growth and skew → 30–40 partitions.
  6. Round to a number with good divisors (e.g., 32 or 48) so partition assignment is even across consumers and brokers.

The over-provisioning is insurance: partitions cannot be removed, and adding them later breaks key ordering. Starting with 2× the immediate need is cheap compared to the migration cost.

Too few partitions

  • Throughput ceiling. The topic cannot exceed the throughput of its partitions' leaders, no matter how many producers or brokers are available.
  • Consumer starvation. Extra consumers sit idle; the group's parallelism is capped.
  • Hot partitions. With few partitions, a skewed key has fewer destinations and concentrates more load on each.
  • No headroom. A traffic spike cannot be absorbed by adding consumers, because there is nothing for them to consume.

Too many partitions

  • Longer rebalances. A rebalance moves partition assignments; more partitions means a larger assignment to compute, distribute, and apply. A 10,000-partition topic can take tens of seconds to rebalance, during which consumption pauses.
  • Higher end-to-end latency. Batching is less effective (fewer messages per partition per batch), and the consumer polls more partitions, adding per-fetch overhead.
  • More broker resources. Each partition consumes file handles, memory for indexes and buffers, and replication bandwidth. Thousands of partitions per broker is a real operational burden.
  • Slower leader elections. A broker failure with 5,000 partition leaders triggers 5,000 elections, which can take minutes to complete and leaves those partitions briefly unavailable.
  • Controller pressure. Even with KRaft, the controller must track metadata for every partition; very large clusters hit scaling limits in metadata propagation.

The partition-to-consumer ratio

A common rule is 2–4 partitions per consumer, so that a consumer failure can be absorbed (its partitions are redistributed) without overloading the remaining consumers, and so that adding a consumer gives a meaningful throughput increase. With 1 partition per consumer, any failure halves throughput; with 100 partitions per consumer, adding a consumer gives little benefit and a failure redistributes too many partitions.

The partition-to-broker ratio

A healthy cluster has partitions spread evenly across brokers, with a replication factor of 3 so each broker hosts a mix of leaders and followers. The total partition count per broker should be in the hundreds to low thousands for most clusters; beyond that, the per-broker overhead (file handles, replication threads, memory) becomes significant. If the topic needs 100,000 partitions, the cluster needs enough brokers to keep each broker's count reasonable.

Partitions and replication cost

Replication cost scales with partition count: each partition has R replicas, each replica is a separate fetch stream, and each ISR is tracked individually. A topic with 1,000 partitions and replication factor 3 has 3,000 replicas, and a broker failure triggers 1,000 leader elections. The overhead is not linear in message rate — it is linear in partition count — so very high partition counts are expensive even at low message rates.

When to add partitions

Add partitions when throughput is capped or consumer parallelism is insufficient. The migration is not free: existing keys move to different partitions (breaking per-key order across the change), so the process is:

  1. Plan the increase during low traffic.
  2. Add partitions to the topic (the broker handles reassignment).
  3. Accept that existing keys may move; consumers that need strict order must reconcile.
  4. Monitor lag and throughput during the transition.

Because the migration is painful, over-provisioning at creation is the preferred strategy. The cost of extra partitions is small compared to the cost of a partition-count change on a live topic.

Trade-off

Partition count is a one-way decision with a long shadow. Too few caps the topic's ceiling; too many adds operational overhead that compounds. The right answer is usually 2× the immediate need, rounded to a number with good divisors, chosen with the maximum expected consumer parallelism in mind.

5

What is the role of the ISR (in-sync replica) list? How does a broker enter or leave the ISR, and what does that mean for durability?

Short Answer

The ISR is the set of replicas that are caught up to the leader's high-water mark. Only ISR members are eligible for leader election, and acks=all waits for the ISR to acknowledge. A replica leaves the ISR when it falls behind the leader by more than replica.lag.time.max.ms; it re-enters when it catches up. The ISR size is the durability guarantee: a partition with ISR size 2 can tolerate one failure.

What the ISR is, precisely

Each partition has one leader and N-1 followers. Followers continuously fetch from the leader. A follower is in the ISR if it has fetched and acknowledged all messages up to the leader's high-water mark within the last replica.lag.time.max.ms (default 10 seconds). The ISR is therefore a dynamic set — it shrinks when a follower falls behind and grows when it catches up. The leader's high-water mark is the highest offset replicated to all ISR members, and it is the boundary below which messages are considered committed.

Entering and leaving the ISR

  • Leaving: a follower that falls behind by more than the lag time threshold (measured in time, not bytes) is removed from the ISR by the leader. This happens when the follower's disk is slow, its network is congested, it is paused by GC, or it has crashed. The removal is reported to the controller, which persists the new ISR.
  • Re-entering: a follower that catches up to the leader's high-water mark rejoins the ISR. It must first fetch all messages it missed, then acknowledge the high-water mark; the leader then adds it back. There is no "partial" state — a replica is either in the ISR or not.

How the ISR relates to durability

The ISR is the mechanism that makes acks=all meaningful. With acks=all, the leader waits for all ISR members to acknowledge before responding to the producer. So when the producer receives an ack, the message is on all ISR members — the durability guarantee. If the leader then fails, a follower from the ISR is promoted and has the message. If a follower had left the ISR, it is not eligible for promotion, and the message is not considered committed until the ISR shrinks to a size where the remaining members have it.

The critical setting is min.insync.replicas (typically 2). If the ISR shrinks below this threshold, the leader rejects writes with NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS — it refuses to accept writes it cannot guarantee durably. This is the trade-off between availability and durability: with ISR=2, one broker failure still allows writes; with ISR=1, no writes are accepted, and the partition is unavailable until a replica catches up.

The ISR shrink and the unclean election

If the ISR shrinks to 1 (only the leader is in sync) and then the leader fails, the partition has no eligible leader — all other replicas are out of sync. The partition becomes unavailable unless unclean.leader.election.enable=true, which allows an out-of-sync replica to be promoted. That restores availability but loses the messages the out-of-sync replica did not have — a data-loss event. For any system with a no-loss requirement, unclean election must be disabled, and the partition is unavailable until an ISR member returns.

The ISR and latency

The ISR size directly affects write latency with acks=all: the leader waits for all ISR members, so the slowest ISR member determines the latency. A follower on a slow disk or a congested network slows every write. This is why ISR health matters: an ISR member that is consistently slow adds latency to every produce, even if it never leaves the ISR. The mitigation is to monitor per-replica fetch lag and replace or repair slow replicas before they degrade the whole partition.

ISR and the high-water mark

The high-water mark is the offset below which all ISR members have the message. Consumers with isolation.level=read_committed read only up to the high-water mark; consumers with read_uncommitted read up to the leader's log end offset (LEO), which may include messages not yet replicated. This is why read-committed consumers are safer: they never see messages that could be lost on a leader failure.

Operational signals

  • UnderReplicatedPartitions: the count of partitions whose ISR is smaller than the replication factor. A non-zero value means a replica is lagging or down. This is the single most important Kafka health metric.
  • ISR shrink rate: how often replicas leave the ISR. A high rate means instability (network, disk, GC).
  • ISR expand rate: how often replicas rejoin. A high rate paired with a high shrink rate means replicas are flapping.
  • Per-replica fetch lag: bytes or time behind the leader. A replica approaching the lag threshold is about to leave the ISR.
Common Pitfall

Setting min.insync.replicas=1 to maximize availability and then expecting no data loss. With that setting, acks=all degrades to acks=1 — the leader acks without any follower having the message — and a leader failure loses the last few writes. The whole point of the ISR is defeated. The minimum for durability is 2.

Part B — Delivery Guarantees & Exactly-Once Acks, idempotence, and transactions

6

What are the different delivery guarantees in Kafka, and how do you achieve exactly-once semantics?

Short Answer

Kafka offers at-most-once (fire and forget), at-least-once (retries with acks, possible duplicates), and exactly-once (idempotent producer plus transactions, or idempotent consumer plus dedup). Exactly-once in Kafka is real for Kafka-to-Kafka pipelines via the transactional API; for Kafka-to-external-system it requires idempotent writes or a transactional outbox. There is no free exactly-once.

At-most-once

The producer sends and does not wait for an ack (acks=0) and does not retry. If the broker is slow or the network drops the message, it is lost. The consumer commits the offset before processing, so a crash loses the message. This is acceptable for metrics, logs, and telemetry — loss-tolerant data — and for nothing else.

At-least-once

The default and most common configuration: acks=all, retries with backoff, and the consumer commits after processing. The producer's retry can duplicate a message if the original write succeeded but the ack was lost; the consumer's crash between processing and commit can reprocess. At-least-once is the right default for most systems, provided consumers are idempotent (dedup by message ID, or upsert semantics).

Idempotent producer

The producer assigns a sequence number to each message per partition. The broker tracks the last sequence number per producer ID per partition. A retried message with a sequence number already seen is deduplicated: the broker returns the existing offset instead of appending again. This eliminates the producer-side duplicate — the most common source of exactly-once violations — at the cost of a small per-batch sequence field and a broker-side dedup cache. Idempotence is the foundation of exactly-once; without it, the transactional layer cannot work.

Transactions

The transactional API extends idempotence to multiple partitions and topics. A transactional producer initializes a transaction, writes to multiple partitions, and commits. The broker's transaction coordinator tracks the transaction's state; consumers with isolation.level=read_committed see the writes only after the commit, and never see the writes of an aborted transaction. The sequence is:

  1. Producer calls beginTransaction().
  2. Producer sends messages to one or more partitions (they are appended but marked as part of the transaction).
  3. Producer sends the consumer offsets it has processed as part of the same transaction (the sendOffsetsToTransaction call).
  4. Producer calls commitTransaction(); the coordinator commits atomically.
  5. Read-committed consumers now see the messages.

If the producer crashes before committing, the transaction times out and is aborted; read-committed consumers never see the messages, and the consumer offsets are not advanced. This is the exact-once guarantee for Kafka-to-Kafka: read from topic A, process, write to topic B, commit offsets — all atomic.

Where exactly-once ends

The transactional API covers Kafka producers and consumers. It does not cover external systems. Writing to a database in a transaction and then producing to Kafka is not atomic — one can succeed and the other fail. The standard solution is the transactional outbox: write the message to an outbox table in the same database transaction as the business change, then a relay reads the outbox and publishes to Kafka. The relay must handle deduplication (by outbox ID) and retry. This gives exactly-once effects across the database-Kafka boundary, at the cost of a relay component and a bit of latency.

Consumer-side exactly-once

A consumer that processes messages and writes to an external system must be idempotent, because the at-least-once delivery is unavoidable for external writes. The patterns are: an idempotency key stored in the target system; an upsert instead of an insert; a dedup table keyed by message ID; or a transaction in the target system that includes the processed-message record. Without one of these, the consumer will eventually write the same result twice.

The cost of exactly-once

Transactions add latency (the commit waits for the coordinator), overhead (the producer maintains transaction state, the coordinator maintains a log), and complexity (transaction timeout tuning, abort handling). They are worth it for financial data, inventory, and anything where a duplicate is a bug. They are overkill for metrics, logs, and feeds. The right answer is per-topic: exactly-once for the ledger, at-least-once with idempotent consumer for the search index, at-most-once for telemetry.

Interview Framing

Say: “Exactly-once for Kafka-to-Kafka via transactions; at-least-once plus an idempotent consumer for anything that writes to an external system; and an outbox if the write originates in a database.” That separates the three boundaries and names the mechanism for each.

How does the acks setting affect durability and latency?

Short Answer

acks=0 gives the lowest latency and no durability — the producer does not wait for any acknowledgement. acks=1 waits for the leader to write locally — moderate latency, but the message can be lost if the leader fails before replication. acks=all waits for all ISR members — highest durability, highest latency. The setting is a direct trade-off between the three.

SettingWaits forDurabilityLatencyUse case
acks=0NothingNone — lost on any failureLowestMetrics, logs, telemetry
acks=1Leader's local writeLost if leader fails before replicationModerateLoss-tolerant event streams
acks=allAll ISR membersNo loss if ISR > min.insync.replicasHighestPayments, inventory, anything that matters

acks=0

The producer sends the message and moves on. It does not wait for the broker to acknowledge, so it has no idea whether the message was written, whether it reached the wrong leader, or whether the broker was down. The latency is just the network send time (often sub-millisecond). The throughput is highest because there is no round trip. The cost is complete loss of durability — any failure loses the in-flight messages, and the producer cannot even detect it. This is appropriate only for data whose loss is acceptable.

acks=1

The producer waits for the leader to append to its local log, then receives an ack. The message is durable on one broker, but not replicated. If the leader crashes before followers fetch it, the message is lost — and because the producer was acked, it will not retry. The latency is one round trip to the leader (a few milliseconds in the same datacenter). The throughput is high because the leader does not wait for followers. This is the historical default, and it is the wrong default for anything that matters.

acks=all (also acks=-1)

The producer waits for all ISR members to acknowledge. The message is durable on every in-sync replica. The latency is the round trip to the leader plus the round trip from the leader to the slowest follower (the ISR's slowest member determines the latency). The throughput is lower because each write waits for replication. This is the only setting that delivers the no-loss guarantee, provided min.insync.replicas is at least 2 and unclean leader election is disabled.

The interaction with min.insync.replicas

acks=all waits for all ISR members, but if the ISR has shrunk to 1 (only the leader), then "all" means the leader only — the same as acks=1. min.insync.replicas prevents this: it is the minimum ISR size below which the leader rejects writes. With min.insync.replicas=2, a leader with ISR=1 refuses writes with NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS rather than accepting them without durability. This is the correct configuration for no-loss: acks=all + min.insync.replicas=2 + unclean.leader.election.enable=false.

Latency in detail

The latency of acks=all is dominated by the slowest ISR member. If one follower has a slow disk or is on a congested network, every write waits for it. This is why a slow replica is a latency problem for the whole partition, not just for that replica. The mitigations: monitor per-replica fetch lag and replace slow replicas; use acks=all with a small ISR (2 or 3) rather than a large one; and consider acks=all only for topics that need it, using acks=1 for the rest.

The throughput interaction

Higher acks means lower throughput per producer because each message waits for a round trip. This is mitigated by batching: the producer sends a batch and waits for one ack, so the round trip is amortized across the batch. With a batch of 100 messages, the per-message latency cost of acks=all is 1/100 of the round trip. This is why high-throughput systems use large batches and acks=all together — they get durability without paying the full latency cost per message.

The durability interaction

The no-loss guarantee requires that the ack is sent only after the message is durable. acks=1 acks before replication, so a leader failure loses the message. acks=all acks after replication, so a leader failure promotes a follower that has the message. The guarantee is not absolute — if all ISR members fail simultaneously (a rack failure), the message is lost even with acks=all, unless the replication is spread across failure domains (racks, AZs). This is why replica placement matters as much as the acks setting.

The producer retry interaction

With acks=all, a failed ack (timeout, leader change) causes a producer retry. Without idempotence, the retry can duplicate a message if the original write succeeded. With idempotence enabled, the retry is deduplicated by the broker's sequence number check. This is why idempotence should always accompany acks=all — the two together give no-loss and no-duplicate, which is what most systems actually want.

Common Pitfall

Setting acks=all and assuming no loss, while leaving min.insync.replicas=1 and unclean.leader.election.enable=true. The acks setting is necessary but not sufficient; the other two settings determine whether the ISR guarantee actually holds. The correct trio is acks=all + min.insync.replicas=2 + unclean election disabled.

How do you achieve exactly-once processing without sacrificing throughput? What role do idempotent producers and transactional consumers play?

Short Answer

The throughput cost of exactly-once comes from the extra round trips (transaction coordination, sequence-number checks) and the read-committed latency (waiting for the high-water mark). You recover the throughput by batching aggressively, keeping transactions short, and confining exactly-once to the topics that need it. The role of the idempotent producer is to eliminate duplicates from retries; the transactional consumer is to make the read-process-write-commit sequence atomic.

Why exactly-once is expensive

Exactly-once requires three additions over at-least-once: idempotent production (sequence numbers and dedup on the broker), transactional writes (a transaction coordinator, a transaction log, a two-phase commit across partitions), and read-committed consumption (waiting for the high-water mark, which adds latency). Each adds overhead: CPU for sequence tracking, network for the coordinator, latency for the commit. Without careful design, throughput can drop by 30–50%.

Idempotent producers

The producer assigns a monotonic sequence number to every message per partition and includes it in the record batch. The broker maintains the last sequence number per producer ID per partition; if a batch arrives with a sequence number less than or equal to the last seen, it is a duplicate and is dropped (the broker returns the original offset). This eliminates the most common duplicate: the retry that follows a lost ack. The overhead is a few bytes per batch and a small in-memory dedup structure on the broker; the throughput cost is negligible.

Idempotence is enabled with enable.idempotence=true, which also forces acks=all and retries > 0. It is the foundation of exactly-once: without it, the transactional layer has nothing to build on.

Transactional producers

A producer with a transactional.id can write to multiple partitions atomically. The producer begins a transaction, writes to topics, optionally sends consumer offsets, and commits. The transaction coordinator (a broker role) tracks the transaction's state in an internal topic (__transaction_state). On commit, the coordinator writes a commit marker to each partition's log; read-committed consumers see the messages only after the marker.

The atomicity covers produce-to-Kafka and commit-offset-to-Kafka in one transaction. This is the exactly-once guarantee for the Kafka-to-Kafka pipeline: the input offsets and the output messages are committed together, so a reprocessing after failure starts from the committed offset and does not duplicate the output.

Read-committed consumers

A consumer with isolation.level=read_committed reads only up to the last stable offset (LSO), which is the high-water mark minus any in-flight transactions. It never sees the messages of a transaction that has not committed, and it does not advance its position past an open transaction. The cost is latency: the consumer waits for the transaction to commit before seeing the messages. For short transactions (a few milliseconds), the latency cost is small; for long transactions (seconds), it is significant.

Recovering throughput

  • Batch aggressively. Larger batches amortize the per-batch sequence overhead and the coordinator round trips. batch.size and linger.ms should be tuned for the throughput target, not the default.
  • Keep transactions short. A transaction that spans thousands of messages holds the LSO open and adds latency for read-committed consumers. A transaction per batch (a few hundred messages) is a better trade-off than a transaction per record or a transaction per hour.
  • Confine exactly-once. Only the topics that need it should be transactional. The high-volume telemetry topic can be at-least-once with idempotent consumers; the payment ledger topic is transactional. Most systems have a small set of topics that genuinely need exactly-once.
  • Use idempotent consumers where possible. If the consumer's write is idempotent (upsert, dedup key), the Kafka transaction is unnecessary — at-least-once plus idempotent write equals exactly-once effects, without the transaction overhead. This is often the better trade-off.

Transactional consumers in Kafka Streams

Kafka Streams uses the transactional API internally: a stream processor reads from input topics, writes to output topics and state stores, and commits the offsets and the output in one transaction. This is exactly-once stream processing without the application having to manage transactions. The cost is the same as any transaction: higher latency, coordinator overhead, and the requirement that input and output are both Kafka.

The external-system boundary

Exactly-once across Kafka and a database requires one of: (1) the transactional outbox (write to the database and the outbox in one transaction, then relay); (2) a two-phase commit between Kafka and the database (rare, complex, and slow); (3) an idempotent write to the database keyed by the Kafka message ID (simplest, but requires the database to enforce the uniqueness). For most systems, the idempotent write or the outbox is the right choice; two-phase commit is almost never worth the complexity.

The throughput reality

In practice, exactly-once with batching and short transactions costs 10–30% throughput compared to at-least-once. For most systems, that is acceptable given the correctness benefit. If the workload is throughput-bound and cannot afford the cost, the alternative is at-least-once plus idempotent consumers, which gives the same end-to-end effect with less overhead — the application just has to be designed for it.

Trade-off

Exactly-once via transactions is the strongest guarantee, but it costs throughput and latency and requires all participants to be Kafka. Idempotent consumers give the same effect for external systems at lower overhead, but require the application to implement deduplication. The right choice is per-boundary: use transactions for Kafka-to-Kafka, idempotent consumers for Kafka-to-external.

Part C — Retention, Compaction & Tiered Storage How long to keep data, and where to keep it

How long should messages be retained? How do you support both short-term hot retention and long-term cold storage?

Short Answer

Hot retention is sized for the consumer's catch-up window (a weekend outage, a slow consumer) — typically 7–30 days. Cold retention extends this to months or years for audit, replay, and new-consumer bootstrapping, using tiered storage (messages moved to object storage as they age). The choice is driven by how long a consumer might lag, how long the business needs history, and how much disk budget the brokers have.

The four retention questions

  1. How long can a consumer lag? The retention must exceed the maximum plausible lag, or the consumer loses data. A consumer that is down for a weekend needs at least 3 days of retention; one that is down for a month needs 30 days or more.
  2. How long does the business need history? Audit, compliance, and reprocessing require longer retention. Financial data may need years; telemetry may need days.
  3. How much disk budget is available? Retention costs storage proportional to throughput × retention time. A topic at 100 MB/s with 7 days of retention uses ~60 TB (before replication); 30 days uses ~260 TB. Replication factor multiplies this.
  4. How is retention enforced? By time (retention.ms), by size (retention.bytes), or both. The smaller of the two limits applies.

Hot retention

Hot retention is the messages kept on the broker's local disk. The broker serves consumers from these messages, using the page cache for recent segments and disk for older ones. The hot retention is sized for the active consumption: the messages a consumer might need to catch up, plus a margin for the slowest consumer. For most topics, 7–30 days of hot retention is sufficient, because a consumer lagging more than that is effectively broken and needs a reset.

The disk budget is the constraint. A broker with 10 TB of disk and replication factor 3 can host about 3.3 TB of unique messages per broker; across 10 brokers, that is 33 TB of unique messages, or about 4 days at 100 MB/s. Longer hot retention requires more brokers, more disk, or tiered storage.

Cold retention

Cold retention is for messages that are older than the hot window but still needed for audit, compliance, or replay. The traditional approach was to copy messages to an external store (S3, HDFS) via a connector (Kafka Connect S3 sink, or a custom job). This works but is operationally heavy: the copy is separate from the broker, the consumer cannot read the cold data through Kafka, and the schema evolution must be handled separately.

The modern approach is tiered storage (Question 11): the broker itself moves old segments to object storage and serves them transparently when a consumer reads from a cold offset. This gives cold retention without a separate copy pipeline, and the consumer sees a single topic regardless of where the messages actually live.

The retention policy choices

PolicyMechanismUse case
Time-based deleteretention.msEvent streams, logs, telemetry
Size-based deleteretention.bytesBounded disk usage, when time is less important
Compactioncleanup.policy=compactChangelogs, state stores, CDC (Question 10)
Compact + deletecleanup.policy=compact,deleteChangelogs that also need a time bound

The retention and consumer interaction

A consumer that falls behind the retention window cannot catch up — the messages it needed are gone. The consumer's committed offset is now below the earliest available offset, and the broker returns an OffsetOutOfRange error. The consumer's auto.offset.reset policy determines what happens: earliest jumps to the earliest available (skipping the lost messages), latest jumps to the end (skipping everything not yet consumed), or none fails the consumer. None of these is a recovery — the messages are simply gone. This is why retention must exceed the maximum plausible consumer lag.

Retention and compliance

Some data must be retained for a fixed period (financial records for 7 years, medical data for 10 years). Kafka is not the right primary store for this — it is a transport and a buffer, not an archive. The archive should be an object store or a database with proper lifecycle management. Kafka's role is to deliver the data to the archive reliably; the archive's role is to keep it. Conflating the two leads to enormous Kafka retention costs and a fragile archive.

The cost curve

Hot retention is expensive: SSD-backed broker disk, replicated 3×, with the operational overhead of a Kafka cluster. Cold retention is cheap: object storage at a fraction of the cost, with lifecycle policies that move it to infrequent-access or archive tiers. The right design keeps the hot window small (days) and the cold window large (months or years), using tiered storage or an external archive to bridge them.

Trade-off

Long hot retention is simple (no separate store) but expensive and operationally heavy. Short hot retention plus tiered storage is cheaper and gives the same consumer experience, at the cost of a more complex storage layer. Short hot retention plus an external archive is cheapest, but the archive is a separate system with its own query semantics and its own operational burden.

What is log compaction? How does it enable event sourcing and CDC without growing unboundedly?

Short Answer

Log compaction retains at least the last value for every key, deleting older values for the same key. It turns a topic into a durable, replayable table: consumers can reconstruct the current state by reading from the beginning, and the log grows with the number of distinct keys, not the number of updates. It is the mechanism behind Kafka's changelog topics, event sourcing, and CDC.

How compaction works

A topic with cleanup.policy=compact is periodically scanned by a cleaner thread. The cleaner reads each partition's segments and identifies, for each key, the latest offset. Older offsets for the same key are removed; the latest is retained. A tombstone (a message with a null value) is retained for a configurable time (delete.retention.ms) to signal deletion to consumers, then removed. The result is a log where each key appears at most once (plus tombstones during the grace period), and the total size is bounded by the number of keys and their value sizes, not by the number of updates.

The compaction is asynchronous and runs in the background. Recent messages (within the last segment) are not compacted until the segment is rolled, so the tail of the log is append-only and uncompacted. Consumers see the full history up to the compaction point, then the compacted tail — which is fine, because a consumer that reads from the beginning and keeps the latest value per key reconstructs the same state regardless of whether old versions are present.

Why this enables event sourcing

Event sourcing stores the sequence of events that produced the current state, rather than the state itself. The events are the source of truth; the state is derived by replaying them. In Kafka, the events are the messages in a compacted topic; the state is reconstructed by a consumer that applies each event to a local store. The compacted topic is a durable event log that is also a state store — it holds every event that has not been superseded, and the latest value for each key is enough to rebuild the state for that key.

The compaction means the event log does not grow with every update: a user's profile updated 1,000 times leaves one value in the compacted topic (plus tombstones), not 1,000. The state can still be reconstructed because the latest event contains the full state (or the last delta, depending on the event design). The cost is that the history of individual updates is lost — for that, you need a separate, non-compacted topic that retains every event.

Why this enables CDC

Change data capture streams database changes (inserts, updates, deletes) into Kafka. Each change is a message keyed by the primary key. A compacted CDC topic holds the latest state of each row, not every change — which is exactly what a downstream service needs to reconstruct a read model. A service that bootstraps from the compacted topic gets the current state of every row, without replaying the entire change history. A service that needs the history reads a non-compacted topic; a service that needs the current state reads the compacted one.

The standard pattern is two topics: a compacted "state" topic for bootstrapping and a non-compacted "events" topic for history. Both are produced by the same CDC connector, and consumers choose the one that matches their need.

Compaction and state stores

Kafka Streams uses compacted topics for its state store changelogs. When a stateful operation (count, aggregate, join) updates a state store, the change is written to a compacted changelog topic. If the stream processor restarts, it replays the changelog to rebuild the state store. Because the changelog is compacted, the replay is bounded by the number of keys, not the number of updates — a stream with millions of updates per key still rebuilds in seconds.

The tombstone handling

A delete in CDC or event sourcing must be represented as a tombstone: a message with the key and a null value. The compacted topic retains the tombstone for delete.retention.ms (default 24 hours) so that consumers see the deletion, then removes it. A consumer that reads from the beginning and replays the tombstones correctly reconstructs the deleted state; a consumer that starts after the tombstone is gone will not see the key at all, which is also correct (the key is deleted). The grace period must be long enough for all consumers to see the tombstone, or a consumer that starts too late will not know the key was deleted.

When compaction is the wrong choice

  • Event streams that need full history: if every event matters (audit, analytics), use delete, not compact. Compaction loses the intermediate events.
  • High-cardinality keys: if every message has a unique key (e.g., a UUID per message), compaction has nothing to remove — each key appears once, so the log grows like a delete topic, but with compaction overhead.
  • Large values: compaction keeps the latest value per key; a topic with multi-megabyte values and many keys retains all of them, which may be too much for the broker disk.
Trade-off

Compaction turns a log into a table, which is powerful for event sourcing and CDC but loses the full history. The standard design is two topics: a compacted state topic for bootstrapping and a non-compacted events topic for history. The consumer chooses based on what it needs.

How do you serve petabyte-scale historical data without running out of disk on the brokers? Explain tiered storage.

Short Answer

Tiered storage moves old log segments from broker disk to object storage (S3, GCS, Azure Blob) and serves reads from object storage transparently when a consumer requests a cold offset. The broker keeps a local cache of recent segments for hot reads, and fetches cold segments on demand. The result is effectively unlimited retention at object-storage cost, without a separate copy pipeline.

The problem

A Kafka cluster's retention is bounded by broker disk. A topic at 100 MB/s with 30 days of retention uses ~260 TB of unique messages; with replication factor 3, that is ~780 TB of broker disk. At $0.10/GB/month for SSD, that is ~$78,000/month — and it scales linearly with throughput and retention. The traditional answer was to copy old messages to S3 via a connector and query the archive separately, which works but creates two systems and two query paths.

How tiered storage works

The broker's log is split into segments. Recent segments are on local disk (the hot tier); older segments are uploaded to object storage (the cold tier) and then removed from local disk. The broker maintains an index mapping offsets to segment locations (local or object), so a consumer reading an old offset triggers a fetch from object storage into the broker's cache, which then serves the consumer. The consumer is unaware of the tiering — it reads from the topic as usual, and the broker handles the cold fetch transparently.

The upload happens asynchronously: a segment is uploaded when it is closed (reaches its size or time limit), and the local copy is removed after the upload is confirmed. The broker keeps a configurable amount of recent data on local disk (the "local retention") and uploads the rest. The cold data is immutable — once uploaded, a segment is never modified — so object storage is a natural fit.

What this enables

  • Effectively unlimited retention: months or years of history at object-storage prices (~$0.02/GB/month vs ~$0.10+ for broker SSD).
  • Consumer catch-up for long outages: a consumer that lagged for weeks can still catch up from the cold tier.
  • New consumer bootstrapping: a new service can read the full history from the cold tier to build its state, without waiting for a separate backfill.
  • Compliance and audit: the full history is available for regulatory queries without keeping it on expensive broker disk.
  • Smaller clusters: the broker disk is sized for the hot tier, not the full retention, which reduces cost and simplifies capacity planning.

The latency trade-off

Reading from object storage is slower than reading from local disk (tens of milliseconds vs single-digit milliseconds), plus the broker's fetch and cache overhead. For real-time consumers, this is invisible — they read from the hot tier. For batch consumers, backfills, or new-consumer bootstrap, the cold reads are slower but happen in parallel and are not latency-sensitive. The design should ensure that the hot tier covers the real-time working set, so the cold tier is only hit by cold reads.

The cache

The broker caches fetched cold segments locally (LRU, bounded by size). A consumer reading a cold range sequentially fetches each segment once; a consumer re-reading the same range hits the cache. The cache size is a tuning parameter: larger cache means more cold reads are fast, at the cost of disk. A common design is to size the cache for the "warm" tier — data that is older than the hot window but still accessed regularly.

The implementations

The disaggregated-storage direction

The logical extreme of tiered storage is disaggregated storage: the brokers hold no persistent data, and everything lives in object storage. WarpStream and similar systems do this — the "broker" is stateless, and all reads and writes go to S3. This makes the broker fleet ephemeral and cheap to scale, at the cost of higher per-operation latency (every read is an S3 GET). It is a different trade-off from tiered storage: tiered storage keeps the hot tier fast and pushes only cold data to S3; disaggregated storage pushes everything to S3 and relies on caching for hot data. The right choice depends on the latency sensitivity and the read/write ratio.

The operational benefits

Beyond cost, tiered storage simplifies operations: brokers can be added or replaced without re-replicating the full history (the cold tier is shared), retention can be extended without adding disk, and the cluster's storage capacity is decoupled from its compute capacity. This is the same principle as separating compute and storage in a data warehouse — it lets each dimension scale independently.

Interview Framing

Say: “I'll use tiered storage to keep the hot window on broker disk and push older segments to S3, so retention is bounded by object-storage cost rather than broker disk. The broker caches recent cold reads so the real-time path is unaffected.” That names the mechanism, the cost model, and the latency mitigation.

Part D — Stream Processing & Operations From raw streams to derived state, and how to keep the cluster healthy

What is Kafka Streams? How does stream processing differ from batch processing in a messaging context?

Short Answer

Kafka Streams is a client library for building stream-processing applications on Kafka: read from input topics, transform, aggregate, join, and write to output topics, with state stored in RocksDB and backed by compacted changelog topics. Stream processing handles unbounded, continuously arriving data with low latency; batch processing handles bounded, historically complete data with high throughput. Kafka can do both — the log is the same, the processing model differs.

Kafka Streams fundamentals

Kafka Streams is not a cluster — it is a library embedded in your application. You start a Streams application, it joins a consumer group, and it processes partitions of the input topics. The parallelism is the partition count: one task per partition, and the application can be scaled by running more instances (up to the partition count). The state is local (RocksDB on disk) and backed by a compacted changelog topic, so a restarted instance rebuilds its state by replaying the changelog.

The processing model

  • Stream: an unbounded sequence of records (a topic).
  • Table: a changelog — the latest value per key (a compacted topic).
  • GlobalKTable: a table replicated to every instance for joins.
  • Operations: map, filter, flatMap, groupByKey, aggregate, count, reduce, join, window, suppress.
  • Windows: tumbling, hopping, sliding, session — for time-based aggregation.
  • State stores: RocksDB-backed, changelog-backed, queryable from outside the app (interactive queries).

Exactly-once in Kafka Streams

Kafka Streams can process exactly-once: the input offsets, the output records, and the state store updates are committed in one Kafka transaction. A failure mid-processing aborts the transaction, the input offsets are not advanced, and the processing restarts from the last committed offset. The state store is consistent with the output because both are part of the same transaction. This is the same transactional API described in Question 8, wrapped in a library so the application does not manage it.

Event time vs processing time

Stream processing distinguishes event time (when the event happened, from the message timestamp) from processing time (when the processor saw it). The difference matters because events arrive late or out of order. Kafka Streams uses watermarks to track event-time progress: a window is closed when the watermark passes its end, and late events within a grace period are still accepted. Without this, a windowed aggregation would be wrong for any event that arrived after its window closed.

Batch processing usually does not have this problem: the batch is complete, and every event is present with its event timestamp. The batch can process in any order because the data is bounded and the aggregation is recomputed from scratch.

Why use stream processing

  • Low latency: a real-time dashboard or alert needs results in seconds, not after the nightly batch.
  • Continuous computation: fraud detection, anomaly detection, and monitoring must react to events as they happen.
  • Incremental state: an aggregation can be maintained incrementally, without reprocessing the full history each time.
  • Downstream freshness: a search index or cache can be updated in near-real-time via CDC and stream processing, instead of waiting for a batch refresh.

When batch is the right choice

  • Full-history recomputation: training a model, rebuilding a warehouse table, or running a complex analytical query that needs all the data.
  • High-throughput bulk processing: processing terabytes of logs or backups where latency does not matter.
  • Complex transformations: joins across many datasets, heavy ML pipelines, or anything that is easier to express as a full-data computation than as an incremental one.

The combination

Most data platforms use both: stream processing for the real-time layer (dashboards, alerts, CDC to search) and batch processing for the analytical layer (daily reports, model training, warehouse rebuilds). Kafka is the bridge: the same topics feed both a stream processor (for real-time views) and a sink to the warehouse (for batch views). The two are not competing — they serve different latency and completeness requirements.

Kafka Streams vs Flink vs Spark Structured Streaming

Kafka Streams is a library, embedded in your app, tightly coupled to Kafka, simple to operate (no cluster). Flink is a cluster-based engine with richer event-time semantics, exactly-once across more systems, and SQL support (Flink SQL). Spark Structured Streaming is a microbatch engine on Spark, with broader connectors and integration with the Spark ecosystem. The choice is Kafka Streams for simple Kafka-native pipelines, Flink for complex event processing with strong event-time semantics, Spark for pipelines that already use Spark.

Trade-off

Stream processing gives low latency and incremental state at the cost of complexity (event time, watermarks, late data, exactly-once). Batch is simpler to reason about and cheaper per record, at the cost of latency. The right choice is driven by the freshness requirement and the complexity budget, not by which is more modern.

Under-replicated partitions

This is the canary. A partition is under-replicated when its ISR is smaller than its replication factor — a follower is behind or down. If it stays at zero, the cluster is healthy. If it is non-zero and not recovering, a broker is down or a network link is saturated. If it is non-zero and recovering, a replica is catching up (normal after a broker restart). The metric should be alerted on as a sustained value, not a transient one.

Offline partitions

A partition with no leader is unavailable — producers and consumers cannot use it. This is the most severe broker metric; it means a partition has lost all ISR members and cannot elect a leader (or unclean election is disabled). Any non-zero value is a page. The causes: all ISR replicas are down, the controller cannot reach them, or the cluster is in a state where leadership cannot be established.

Active controller count

The cluster must have exactly one active controller. Zero means no controller (metadata changes fail, leader elections stall); more than one means a split-brain in the controller election (a serious bug or a network partition). Both are pages.

Consumer lag

Lag is the difference between the producer's offset and the consumer's committed offset, per partition, summed or maxed across the group. It is the primary consumer metric. A growing lag means the consumer is not keeping up; a stable lag means steady state; a lag that exceeds the retention window means the consumer will lose data. The signal should be a trend (lag growing for N minutes) plus an absolute threshold (lag approaching the retention limit).

Lag should be monitored per consumer group, per topic, and per partition. A group that looks healthy in aggregate may have one partition with enormous lag (a hot partition or a stuck consumer). The max lag per partition is often more useful than the sum.

Rebalance rate

A high rebalance rate means the consumer group is unstable — consumers joining, leaving, or being kicked out. The causes: consumers taking too long to process a batch (exceeding max.poll.interval.ms), network issues, or a deployment that restarts consumers. A rebalance storm can stop consumption entirely, so a high rate is a page. The mitigation: static group membership, cooperative sticky protocol, smaller batches, and a longer poll interval.

Producer metrics

  • Record send rate and error rate: the basic throughput and failure signals.
  • Request latency: the p99 of the produce request. A rising p99 means the brokers are saturated or the ISR is slow.
  • Record retry rate: high retries indicate broker or network issues; combined with idempotence, they are safe, but a rising rate is a symptom.
  • Batch size and compression ratio: tuning signals — small batches or poor compression indicate misconfiguration.

Cluster metadata and controller

The controller's health is critical for metadata operations (topic creation, leader election, partition reassignment). A slow controller delays every metadata change, which manifests as slow leader elections and slow client metadata refreshes. The metrics: controller request queue size, controller event queue time, and metadata propagation lag across brokers.

End-to-end monitoring

The cluster metrics are necessary but not sufficient. A healthy cluster can serve a broken pipeline if the producer stopped producing or the consumer is silently failing. The end-to-end signals: the producer's throughput (should match the business event rate), the consumer's processing latency, the end-to-end latency (produce timestamp to consume timestamp), and the dead-letter queue depth. These are application-level, not broker-level, and they catch what the broker metrics miss.

The dashboards and alerts

A standard Kafka dashboard has four panels: cluster health (under-replicated, offline, controller), broker resources (CPU, network, disk, request queues), consumer groups (lag, rebalance rate, throughput), and producer (throughput, error rate, latency). Alerts are on the pages (offline partitions, controller count, sustained under-replicated) and on the trends (lag growing, rebalance rate rising, disk approaching threshold). Per-partition alerts catch hotspots that aggregate metrics hide.

Common Pitfall

Monitoring only aggregate consumer lag. A group with one partition lagging by millions of messages looks healthy in the sum if the other partitions are at zero. The max lag per partition is the metric that catches the problem, and it should be alerted on separately.

Compare Kafka, Pulsar, and Kinesis. When would you choose each?

Short Answer

Kafka is the de facto standard: mature, high-throughput, huge ecosystem, self-managed or managed. Pulsar separates compute and storage (brokers and bookies), supports multi-tenancy natively, and offers tiered storage by default. Kinesis is AWS-managed, simple to operate, but with AWS-specific constraints (shard limits, retention, throughput). Choose Kafka for control and ecosystem, Pulsar for multi-tenancy and disaggregated storage, Kinesis for AWS-native simplicity.

Kafka

Kafka is the default for most event-streaming use cases. Its strengths: the largest ecosystem (Connect, Streams, ksqlDB, Schema Registry, a huge connector library), the most mature operational tooling, the most third-party integrations, and a proven track record at scale. Its architecture — brokers store and serve, controller manages metadata — is simple to understand and has been refined over a decade.

The weaknesses: storage and compute are coupled (adding storage means adding brokers), multi-tenancy is basic, and the operational burden is significant for self-managed deployments. Confluent Cloud and MSK address the operational burden; KRaft and tiered storage address the scaling limits. For most teams, Kafka is the right answer because the ecosystem and tooling reduce the total cost of ownership even when a competitor is technically better on a specific axis.

Pulsar

Pulsar's distinguishing feature is the separation of compute and storage: brokers handle publishing and consuming, while BookKeeper bookies store the messages. This means storage scales independently of compute — adding storage means adding bookies, not adding brokers — and it enables native multi-tenancy, geo-replication, and tiered storage. Pulsar was designed for the multi-tenant, geo-distributed platform use case, and it does that better than Kafka.

The weaknesses: the ecosystem is smaller than Kafka's, the operational complexity is higher (brokers, bookies, ZooKeeper — though the newer versions reduce this), and the community is smaller. For a team that needs multi-tenancy, geo-replication, and disaggregated storage as first-class features, Pulsar is a strong choice; for a team that needs the broadest ecosystem and the most battle-tested operations, Kafka is safer.

Kinesis

Kinesis is AWS-managed, which means no brokers, no partitions, no ZooKeeper — just a stream and a shard count. It integrates natively with Lambda, Firehose, Analytics, and the rest of AWS. For a team already on AWS with a simple streaming need, Kinesis is often the fastest path: create a stream, configure a Lambda, done.

The weaknesses: shards are the unit of capacity and cost, and resharding is manual (split or merge shards). There is no exactly-once (at-least-once only), no transactions, and the retention is limited (up to 365 days, with the extended retention add-on). The lock-in is real — migrating off Kinesis means re-architecting the producers and consumers. For AWS-native pipelines with modest scale and no need for exactly-once, Kinesis is fine; for anything that needs scale, transactions, or portability, Kafka is the better choice.

The decision framework

  1. Are you AWS-native with a simple need? Kinesis (lowest operational burden).
  2. Do you need multi-tenancy, geo-replication, or disaggregated storage as first-class? Pulsar.
  3. Do you need the broadest ecosystem, the most integrations, or exactly-once? Kafka.
  4. Do you have a dedicated platform team? Self-managed Kafka or Pulsar; otherwise managed Kafka.
  5. Is the workload latency-sensitive with small messages? Kinesis (managed) or a managed Kafka; Pulsar is competitive.

The managed-cloud reality

In practice, the choice is often between managed services: Confluent Cloud (Kafka), MSK (Kafka), StreamNative (Pulsar), and Kinesis. The managed service removes the operational burden, which is often the dominant factor. For a team without a streaming platform group, a managed Kafka or Kinesis is the pragmatic choice; Pulsar's benefits are largest when the team is large enough to operate it or when the multi-tenancy requirement is real.

Trade-off

Kafka's ecosystem and maturity make it the safe default; Pulsar's architecture is technically superior for multi-tenancy and disaggregated storage, but the ecosystem and operational familiarity are smaller; Kinesis's zero-operations model is compelling for AWS-native teams, at the cost of lock-in, scale limits, and no exactly-once. The right choice depends on the team's constraints as much as the technology's features.

Interview Framing

Say: “Kafka is the default because of the ecosystem and exactly-once. Pulsar if multi-tenancy or disaggregated storage is a first-class requirement. Kinesis if we're AWS-native and the operational burden matters more than the feature set.” That names each platform's differentiator and the decision criterion.
