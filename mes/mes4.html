Part A — Architecture & Data Model The pipeline, the components, and where preferences live

1

Design a notification system that delivers across push (APNs/FCM), email, SMS, and in-app channels for 100 million DAU.

Short Answer

The system is a fan-out pipeline with a policy layer in the middle. Business events enter a queue; a notification service resolves the audience, checks preferences, renders templates, and decides which channels to use; per-channel adapters enqueue to providers (APNs, FCM, SES/SendGrid, Twilio) and record delivery receipts. At 100M DAU, the design is dominated by provider rate limits and by the preference lookup at fan-out time, not by raw storage.

Scale arithmetic first

Assume 100M DAU and 10 notifications per user per day — a mix of transactional (2) and promotional (8). That is 1B notifications/day ≈ 11,600/s average. Promotional campaigns are bursty: a single campaign to 50M users in a 30-minute window is ~28,000/s sustained, and a "breaking news" push to 20M users in 5 minutes is ~67,000/s. The design must handle the burst, not the average, and must never let a promotional burst delay a transactional OTP.

Components

ComponentResponsibility
Event ingestionReceives business events (order placed, payment failed, login from new device) from services via a queue or API

The pipeline

  1. Business event → queue. A service publishes a notification event (type, user/audience, payload) to a topic. The queue decouples the producer from the delivery latency and absorbs bursts.
  2. Orchestrator consumes. For each event, it resolves the recipient list (single user, segment, or all-users). For segment campaigns, the audience is precomputed and fanned out in chunks.
  3. Preference check. For each recipient and channel, look up preferences (cached). If opted out of that channel for that category, drop it for that channel.
  4. Rate cap check. Ask the rate limiter whether this user may receive another notification of this category on this channel today. Promotional is capped; transactional is not.
  5. Template render. Fetch the template for the type/channel/locale and render with the payload. Validation errors fail the notification loudly, not silently.
  6. Channel selection. The policy decides: push if the app is installed and the token is valid; email if push is unavailable or the user prefers email; SMS for critical or for users without the app; in-app always (the inbox is the durable record).
  7. Enqueue per channel. Each channel has its own queue and worker pool, with its own rate limit, retry policy, and priority. This isolation is what prevents a promotional burst from starving transactional messages.
  8. Provider send. The adapter calls the provider API, handles the response, and records the receipt (accepted, invalid token, throttled, error).
  9. Delivery tracking. APNs/FCM return acceptance, not delivery. Delivery receipts come from the device or the provider's feedback channel (APNs 410 for expired tokens, FCM's delivery reports). Opens come from the app or tracking pixels for email.

Idempotency

Every notification carries a notification_id derived from the business event (e.g., order-123-confirmation). The orchestrator checks a dedup store before sending; if the ID exists, the send is skipped. This is essential because event queues deliver at-least-once, and a retried event must not send a second "your order shipped" push. The dedup store is a keyed cache with a TTL (e.g., 7 days), large enough to cover any plausible retry window.

Why channel adapters are separate

Each provider has different failure modes, rate limits, and latency. APNs is fast but has per-connection throughput limits; FCM has per-project quotas; email providers have sending reputation and bounce handling; SMS has per-country carrier rules and cost per message. Putting each behind its own adapter with its own queue, retry policy, and circuit breaker means a failure in one channel does not affect the others, and each can be tuned independently.

Trade-off

Channels could be tried in sequence (push, then email if push fails) or in parallel. Sequential is cheaper (no duplicate sends) but slower and depends on delivery detection, which push does not reliably provide. Parallel is faster but sends multiple channels for the same event, which users find annoying. The pragmatic design is parallel for critical messages (accept the duplication) and sequential for promotional (accept the delay).

Interview Framing

Open with: “This is a fan-out pipeline with a policy layer — preferences, rate caps, and channel selection in the middle. Each channel has its own queue and adapter so a promotional burst can't starve a transactional message.” That names the architecture and the key isolation property in one sentence.

2

What are the core components of a notification service? Walk through the flow from a business event to a delivered notification.

Short Answer

The flow has six stages: ingestion, audience resolution, policy decision, rendering, dispatch, and tracking. Each stage is a separate component so it can be scaled, tested, and failed independently. The critical insight is that the notification service does not know what a "good" notification is — it trusts the policy layer and the template service — so the business logic lives in configuration, not code.

Stage 1 — Ingestion

A business service publishes an event: {type: "order_shipped", user_id, order_id, payload}. It does not call the notification service directly — it publishes to a topic. The notification service consumes the topic. This decouples the producer from the notification latency and lets the notification service be unavailable without blocking the business flow. For direct sends (e.g., an OTP the user is waiting for), a synchronous API exists as well, but it still enqueues internally and returns an accepted status rather than blocking on the send.

Stage 2 — Audience resolution

For a single-user notification (order shipped, password reset), the audience is one user. For a segment campaign (all users in a region, all users who abandoned a cart), the audience is precomputed by a segmentation job and stored as a list or a query. For an all-user campaign, the audience is the full user base — and this is the case that breaks naive designs (Question 9). The resolution stage materializes the recipient list into chunks (e.g., 10,000 users per chunk) and enqueues each chunk as a fan-out task.

Stage 3 — Policy decision

For each recipient, the policy layer decides:

  • Is the user opted in? Check the preference store per channel per category.
  • Is the user over the rate cap? Check the token bucket for this category on this channel.
  • Is it quiet hours? Check the user's timezone and quiet-hours preference. If yes, defer to the next allowed time (for non-urgent) or send anyway (for critical).
  • Which channels? Push if the device token is valid and the app is installed; email if the user prefers it or push is unavailable; SMS if the notification is critical or the user has no app; in-app always.

This is the most expensive stage per recipient because it does three lookups (preferences, rate limit, device tokens). At 100M users, all three must be cached. The preference and token stores are read-heavy and change rarely, so they are cached in Redis with a few-minute TTL; the rate limiter is in Redis itself.

Stage 4 — Rendering

The template is fetched by (type, channel, locale, version) and rendered with the payload. For push, the template produces a title, body, and deep-link; for email, the full HTML; for SMS, a short text. The rendering is deterministic and side-effect-free, so it can be cached for a short time per template version. A rendering error (missing field, invalid template) fails the notification loudly — it must not silently drop.

Stage 5 — Dispatch

Each channel has its own dispatcher:

ChannelProviderLatencyReliability
Push (iOS)APNs~100 msBest-effort; silent push throttled
Push (Android)FCM~100–500 msBest-effort; Doze delays
In-appWebSocket / inboxImmediate if onlineDurable in the inbox
EmailSES, SendGridSeconds to minutesHigh, but spam filtering
SMSTwilio, SNSSecondsHigh, but carrier filtering

The dispatcher calls the provider API with retries. The provider returns an acceptance (APNs: 200 if accepted for delivery), not a delivery receipt. Delivery tracking is a separate stage.

Stage 6 — Tracking

Each send produces a receipt with status and error code. For push, the provider's feedback channel reports expired tokens (APNs 410 Unregistered, FCM UNREGISTERED), which must be removed from the token store. For email, the provider reports bounces and complaints, which must update the user's deliverability status. For SMS, the carrier reports delivery. The tracking store is time-series, queried for analytics and for the escalation logic in Question 8.

The synchronous vs asynchronous boundary

A critical design decision is where to put the boundary between the business caller and the notification pipeline. For OTPs, the caller needs the notification to be sent within a second; the API must be synchronous up to the enqueue and the provider send, or the OTP is useless. For promotional, the caller does not care; the enqueue and return are enough. The notification service therefore has two API paths: a "send now, wait for provider acceptance" path (with a short timeout) and a "enqueue and return" path. Mixing them in one API is a common source of slowness for OTPs.

Common Pitfall

Blocking the business flow on the provider call. A checkout that waits for the confirmation email before returning slows every purchase and fails when the email provider is slow. The email must be enqueued, not awaited. OTPs are the one legitimate exception, and even they should have a bounded timeout and a fallback.

3

How do you handle user notification preferences and opt-outs across channels? Where is that data stored and how is it consulted?

Short Answer

Preferences are a per-user, per-channel, per-category set of flags, plus quiet hours and timezone, stored in a keyed store sharded by user_id and cached in Redis. They are consulted at send time, not at event time, because preferences can change between the event and the delivery. Opt-outs must be durable, timestamped, and auditable — they are a legal requirement under GDPR and CAN-SPAM, not just a UX preference.

The preference matrix

A user has preferences across three dimensions:

  • Channel: push, email, SMS, in-app, (sometimes voice).
  • Category: transactional (order updates, security), account (password, billing), promotional (deals, newsletter), social (follows, likes), system (maintenance).
  • Granularity: sometimes per notification type (e.g., "order shipped" but not "cart reminder").

The full matrix is large (4 channels × 20 categories × 100 types), but most cells are defaulted. The store holds only the deviations from default, which keeps it small and fast.

Legal categories cannot be opted out of

Transactional messages — a password reset, a fraud alert, a legally required notice — cannot be opted out of for the channels that are legally required to deliver them. The preference UI typically shows them as "required" or greys them out, and the backend enforces the rule: if the notification type is marked mandatory, the preference check is skipped (but the rate limiter and quiet hours still apply unless the type is marked urgent).

Storage

StoreKeyContentsAccess pattern
Preference DBuser_idOpt-out flags, quiet hours, timezone, consent recordsRead at send time; write on user action
Redis cacheprefs:{user_id}Materialized preference document with TTL 5 minRead-heavy; invalidated on write
Consent log(user_id, channel, timestamp)Immutable record of every opt-in/opt-out with IP, source, timestampAppend-only; queried for audits
Suppression listemail / phoneHard bounces, complaints, legal opt-outsChecked before every send

The preference DB is sharded by user_id and replicated. The Redis cache is the hot path: a notification send does a single MGET for the preference document, a device token lookup, and a rate counter — three Redis calls. At 50k notifications/s, that is 150k Redis ops/s, which is comfortably within a sharded Redis cluster's capacity.

Consultation at send time, not event time

This is a subtle but important design decision. If the preference is checked when the event is produced (e.g., at order placement), a user who opts out in the next hour will still receive the notification. If it is checked at send time (just before the provider call), the most recent preference is honored. The cost is one lookup per send, which the cache makes cheap. The benefit is correctness — the user's current choice is respected.

The exception is fan-out: for a segment campaign, checking preferences per recipient at send time is unavoidable, but the audience was resolved earlier. A user who opted out after the audience was built will still be in the list; the send-time check is what filters them out. This is why the send-time check is not optional.

Quiet hours and timezones

Quiet hours are a per-user preference (e.g., no notifications between 22:00 and 08:00 local time) plus a platform-level default. The user's timezone is stored in the preference record (or inferred from the device). At send time, if the notification is non-urgent and the local time is within quiet hours, the notification is deferred to the next allowed time (scheduled, Question 10) rather than dropped. Urgent notifications (fraud alerts, OTPs) bypass quiet hours.

Preference change propagation

When a user changes a preference in the app, the write goes to the preference DB, the Redis cache is invalidated, and a change event is published. Any service that maintains a local cache of preferences subscribes to the change event. For most systems, the Redis TTL (5 minutes) is enough — a stale preference for five minutes is acceptable for promotional, but not for opt-outs. Opt-outs should bypass the TTL: the write invalidates the cache immediately, and the next read fetches the new value.

Suppression lists

Some addresses and numbers are suppressed globally, regardless of user preference: hard-bounced emails, complained emails, reported spam numbers, and legal opt-outs. The suppression list is checked before every send and takes precedence over everything else. It is maintained by the email/SMS provider's feedback and by the compliance team, and it is replicated to every region for low-latency checks.

The audit requirement

Every opt-in and opt-out must be recorded with a timestamp, the source (app, web, support call), the IP if applicable, and the exact text the user agreed to. This is a legal requirement: if a user complains they never opted in, the company must be able to prove they did. The consent log is append-only, retained for the legally required period, and queryable by user. It is not a preference — it is evidence.

Common Pitfall

Checking preferences only at campaign creation. A user who unsubscribes after the campaign is built will still receive it, which is a compliance violation and an unsubscribe-button complaint. The check must be at send time, immediately before the provider call.

How do you design priority queues for notifications? How do transactional alerts differ from promotional campaigns in latency and delivery guarantees?

Short Answer

Split the pipeline by priority: transactional notifications (OTP, fraud alert, order confirmation) go through a dedicated high-priority queue with reserved provider capacity and a sub-second latency target; promotional notifications go through a separate low-priority queue that is throttled, batched, and allowed to wait. The two must not share a queue, a worker pool, or a provider connection, or a promotional burst will delay an OTP.

The latency and guarantee tiers

TierExamplesLatency targetDelivery guaranteeRate cap
Critical OTP, fraud alert, security alert, payment failure < 1 s to provider acceptance Escalate to SMS/email/voice if push not acked Never rate-limited
Transactional Order shipped, booking confirmed, password reset < 5 s At-least-once; durable inbox record Never rate-limited
Account Welcome, billing, subscription change < 60 s At-least-once; retry Soft cap
Promotional Deals, newsletter, re-engagement, recommendations Minutes to hours Best-effort; drop on failure Hard cap (e.g., 2–5/day)
Bulk / marketing Campaign to a segment Hours Best-effort; scheduled windows Segment-level cap

Separate queues, separate everything

The critical design decision is that priority is not a field in a shared queue — it is a separate queue with separate consumers, separate workers, separate rate limits, and (where possible) separate provider connections. A shared queue with a priority field still has head-of-line blocking if the consumers are shared: a slow promotional message holds the worker, and the OTP behind it waits. Separate queues mean the OTP has its own workers and is never behind a promotional message.

The cost is more infrastructure: more queues, more consumers, more connections, more dashboards. The benefit is that a promotional campaign cannot affect transactional latency, which is a hard business requirement — an OTP that arrives in 10 seconds is an OTP the user has already given up on.

Provider connection isolation

APNs and FCM limit throughput per connection or per project. If promotional and transactional notifications share a connection, the promotional burst consumes the throughput and the transactional messages queue behind it. The fix is separate provider credentials or separate connections: one for critical, one for the rest. Where the provider does not support separate quotas, the notification service must partition its own traffic — send critical first, throttle promotional to the remaining capacity.

Reserved capacity

Even with separate queues, the downstream provider has a total capacity. The design must reserve a fraction for critical traffic: the promotional workers are rate-limited to (capacity − reserved), and the critical workers can use up to (reserved + their own). This guarantees that critical traffic always has headroom, even during a promotional peak. The reservation is tuned from production data — the observed critical peak plus a margin.

Batching and the latency trade-off

Promotional notifications are batched: one "5 new deals" push instead of five separate pushes. Batching raises throughput and reduces user annoyance, at the cost of latency (the first deal does not notify until the batch window closes). For critical and transactional, batching is unacceptable — the user needs the OTP now, not in five minutes. So the batch window is a per-tier setting: 0 for critical, seconds for transactional, minutes for promotional.

Backpressure and dropping

When the promotional queue grows beyond capacity, the correct response is to drop or defer, not to expand the queue indefinitely. A promotional notification that arrives after the deal expired is worse than no notification. The promotional workers should drop messages older than a freshness window (e.g., 1 hour) rather than deliver stale ones. Critical and transactional messages never expire this way — they are delivered even if late, or escalated through another channel.

The priority inversion risk

A subtle failure: a shared dependency (the preference store, the template service, the rate limiter) becomes the bottleneck, and the priority queues do not help because both are waiting on the same dependency. The mitigation is to give critical traffic its own path through shared dependencies — a separate connection pool, a separate cache namespace, a separate rate limiter instance — so a promotional storm on the preference store does not slow the critical path. This is the same bulkhead principle as in Volume 9 (Fault Tolerance).

Trade-off

Separate queues and reserved capacity give strong isolation but cost infrastructure and reduce promotional throughput (because part of the capacity is reserved for critical). The alternative — one queue with priorities — is simpler but does not provide real isolation, because the consumers and dependencies are still shared. For a system where OTP latency matters, the isolation is worth the cost.

Interview Framing

Say: “Critical and promotional are separate queues with separate workers and separate provider connections, plus reserved capacity for critical. A shared queue with a priority field still has head-of-line blocking because the workers and dependencies are shared.” That names the isolation property and why a priority field is not enough.

5

What is your retry policy for failed deliveries? How do you avoid retry storms?

Short Answer

Retry only on transient errors (5xx, timeouts, throttling), with exponential backoff and jitter, a bounded number of attempts, and a dead-letter queue after exhaustion. Never retry permanent errors (invalid token, bad address, hard bounce) — those must update the user record, not retry. Retry storms are prevented by jitter, per-provider circuit breakers, retry budgets, and token invalidation on first hard failure.

Classify the error first

Error typeExamplesRetry?Action
Transient (provider)500, 503, timeout, connection resetYesExponential backoff, jitter
Throttling429, provider quota exceededYesBackoff respecting Retry-After
Invalid token / addressAPNs 410, FCM UNREGISTERED, hard bounceNoRemove token, mark address invalid
Malformed request400, invalid payload, template errorNoDead-letter, alert
Authentication401, expired credentialNo (but refresh)Refresh credential, then retry once
User opt-out / suppressionPreference changed, suppressedNoDrop silently, record

The first rule is that retrying a permanent error is not just wasteful — it is harmful. Retrying an invalid token hammers the provider and keeps the token in the store, so the next notification fails the same way. Invalid tokens must be removed on the first hard failure, not retried. This single rule eliminates the majority of retry waste.

The retry policy

  • Exponential backoff: 1s, 5s, 30s, 5min — the delay grows exponentially so a provider outage is not hammered by every client.
  • Jitter: add randomness (±20%) to each delay so a batch of failed sends does not retry in lockstep. Without jitter, a thousand failed sends retry at the same second and create a second spike.
  • Max attempts: 3–5 for push, 5–8 for email/SMS (which have more transient failures). Beyond that, the message is dead-lettered and the sender is notified.
  • Total timeout: a maximum time (e.g., 15 minutes) after which the notification is abandoned even if attempts remain. This prevents a message from being retried for hours after it is no longer relevant.
  • Dead-letter queue: after exhaustion, the message goes to a DLQ for inspection. A notification that never sent is a business problem that must be visible, not silently dropped.

The retry storm

A retry storm happens when a provider has an outage, every notification fails, every notification retries, and the retries themselves become a load spike that prevents recovery. The classic case: APNs returns 503 for ten minutes; 100,000 notifications fail; each retries every second; the retry traffic is 100,000/s against a provider that is already struggling. When APNs recovers, the retry storm hits it all at once and knocks it over again.

The mitigations, in order of importance:

  1. Circuit breaker: after N consecutive failures, stop calling the provider entirely and queue messages locally. Probe occasionally (one request every 30s) until it succeeds, then ramp back up gradually.
  2. Jitter: prevents synchronized retries; the retries spread over the backoff window.
  3. Retry budget: cap the total retry volume as a fraction of successful sends (e.g., 10%). When the budget is exhausted, new retries are dropped and the messages go to the DLQ. This bounds the retry load regardless of the failure rate.
  4. Per-provider limits: the adapter has its own rate limit, so even a retry storm cannot exceed the configured ceiling.
  5. Backoff amplification: when the failure rate is high, increase the base backoff for all retries — the system backs off as a whole, not per message.

Priority-aware retries

Critical notifications (OTP, fraud alert) should retry aggressively; promotional should retry lazily or not at all. The retry policy is per-tier: critical gets 5 attempts with a 500ms base, promotional gets 1 attempt with a 60s delay, and bulk marketing gets no retry (drop and move on). This matches the business value — a missed OTP is a customer who cannot log in; a missed promotional push is a deal the user did not see, which is the same as not sending it.

The escalation interaction

For critical notifications, a failed push is not the end — it is the trigger for escalation (Question 8). The retry policy for push is short (one or two attempts), and the escalation ladder takes over: if push is not acknowledged within N seconds, send SMS; if SMS is not acknowledged, send email; if email is not opened, make a voice call. The retry policy and the escalation ladder must be designed together, or the system retries a channel that is already known to be broken while the user waits.

Observability

The retry policy is only as good as its visibility. The metrics: retry rate per provider, retry success rate, DLQ depth, time to exhaustion, and the distribution of error codes. A rising retry rate is an early warning of a provider degradation; a DLQ with a specific error code is a signal to fix the root cause (invalid template, bad token format). Without these, retries are invisible until the DLQ is full and someone notices.

Common Pitfall

Retrying invalid tokens. APNs returns 410, FCM returns UNREGISTERED, and the naive retry loop tries again and again. The token is permanently invalid — retrying is pure waste and keeps the bad token in the store. The correct action is to remove the token on the first 410, not to retry.

6

How would you handle a third-party push provider (APNs/FCM) outage? What is the fallback strategy?

Short Answer

Detect the outage via error rate and latency, open the circuit breaker, queue notifications locally, and fall back to other channels for critical messages (SMS, email, in-app). APNs and FCM cannot substitute for each other — they are per-platform — so the fallback is cross-channel, not cross-provider. On recovery, ramp traffic back gradually and drain the queue in priority order.

Detection

An outage is not always a clean error. It can manifest as:

  • High error rate: 5xx responses, connection refused, TLS failures.
  • High latency: requests that normally take 100ms now take 10s. This is a gray failure — the provider is up but degraded, and the notification service's threads are tied up waiting.
  • Silent failure: APNs accepts the request (200) but the device never receives it. This is the hardest case, because the provider's response says everything is fine. Detection requires device-side delivery receipts or a feedback channel.
  • Quota exhaustion: FCM returns 429 or a quota-exceeded error, which is a provider-side limit rather than an outage but has the same effect.

The detection signal is a moving window of error rate and p99 latency per provider. A threshold (e.g., 5% error rate over 60 seconds) opens the circuit breaker. The window and threshold must be tuned to avoid flapping on transient blips.

The circuit breaker

When the breaker is open, the adapter stops calling the provider and returns a "deferred" status. The notification is not lost — it is queued (or escalated) — and the system stops generating load against a provider that is already struggling. The breaker probes every 30 seconds with a single request; when the probe succeeds, it moves to half-open and allows a small fraction of traffic; when that succeeds, it closes and resumes full traffic.

Fallback by channel, not by provider

APNs is the only provider for iOS push, and FCM is the only provider for Android push (and for web push in most browsers). There is no alternative push provider to fall back to. The fallback is therefore a different channel:

Notification tierPrimaryFallback during push outage
Critical (OTP, fraud)PushSMS immediately, then email, then voice
Transactional (order, booking)PushIn-app inbox + email; SMS for high-value
AccountPushIn-app inbox + email
PromotionalPushDefer; drop if the offer expires

The in-app inbox is the most reliable fallback: it is served by the application's own backend, not a third-party provider, so it is available whenever the app is. The user sees the notification the next time they open the app. For critical messages, the inbox is not enough because the user may not open the app; that is why the escalation ladder for critical goes to SMS and voice.

Queuing during the outage

Notifications that cannot be sent because the provider is down are queued per channel with a priority order. The queue has a bounded size and a freshness window: a promotional notification older than its freshness window is dropped, not queued indefinitely. Critical and transactional notifications are queued and retried when the breaker closes. The queue depth is a monitored metric — a growing queue during an outage is expected; a queue that exceeds the retention of the underlying store is a data-loss risk.

Recovery: the ramp

When the provider recovers, the queued notifications do not all fire at once. That would be a self-inflicted retry storm against a provider that just came back. The adapter ramps: it sends at 10% of normal rate for the first minute, then 25%, then 50%, then 100%, watching the error rate at each step. If the error rate rises, it backs off again. This gradual ramp is the difference between a recovery and a second outage.

Graceful degradation of non-critical channels

During an outage, the system should disable or delay non-critical notifications to conserve the provider's remaining capacity for critical ones. The promotional queue is paused entirely, the account queue is throttled, and only critical and transactional traffic is allowed. This is a policy decision made at outage time (or automatically by the circuit breaker), and it must be reversible when the provider recovers.

Communication and runbooks

An APNs or FCM outage is a known, documented failure mode. The runbook must specify: how to confirm the outage (status pages, error rates), how to enable the fallback (feature flag for escalation, SMS allow-list), how to communicate to the business (support team, status page), and how to recover (the ramp). The on-call engineer should not be designing the fallback during the incident.

The multi-provider question

For email and SMS, multiple providers exist (SES, SendGrid, Mailgun for email; Twilio, SNS, Vonage for SMS). The adapter can be configured with a primary and a fallback provider, and the circuit breaker switches between them. This is a genuine redundancy that push does not have. The cost is two integrations to maintain and potentially two contracts; the benefit is that a single provider outage does not stop email or SMS delivery.

Common Pitfall

Ramping at full speed when the provider recovers. The queued notifications all fire at once, the provider is overwhelmed again, and the outage repeats. The recovery must be a controlled ramp with monitoring, not a queue drain at full rate.

Part C — Rate Caps, Guarantees, Fan-Out & Scheduling Protecting the user and the system at scale

7

How do you set a cap on promotional pushes per user? How do you enforce it across multiple services?

Short Answer

Use a centralized rate limiter with per-user, per-category token buckets in Redis, enforced at send time by every service that sends notifications. The cap is a policy (e.g., 2 promotional per day, 10 per week), not a hard-coded number, and it is applied after the preference check and before the provider call. Without a centralized limiter, each service's local cap is meaningless because the user's total is the sum across services.

Why per-service caps fail

Suppose the marketing service caps at 1 push/day, the recommendations service caps at 1/day, and the re-engagement service caps at 1/day. Each is under its cap; the user receives 3/day. The cap the user agreed to (and the cap the product promised) is a per-user total, not a per-service total. Only a shared limiter can enforce it.

The limiter design

DimensionExampleStorage
Per user, per category, per day2 promotional/dayRedis key rl:{user}:{cat}:{day}, TTL 48h
Per user, per category, per week10 promotional/weekRedis key with week bucket, TTL 8d
Per user, per channel, per hour5 push/hourToken bucket in Redis
Per user, global20 notifications/day across all categoriesRedis key, TTL 48h
Per segment, per campaign1 per campaign per userDedup key per campaign

The limiter exposes a single API: check_and_increment(user_id, category, channel) → allowed | denied. It is an atomic operation (Lua script or Redis INCR with check), so two concurrent notifications for the same user cannot both pass when only one slot remains. The TTL is the bucket window — a day bucket expires after 48 hours so the key does not leak.

Enforcement point

The check must happen at send time, immediately before the provider call, in the notification service — not at the campaign creation, not at the event production. This is the only place where all notification sources converge, and therefore the only place where a per-user total can be enforced. If a service bypasses the notification service and calls the provider directly, the cap is broken; the architecture must make the notification service the only path to the provider.

Transactional vs promotional

Transactional notifications are not rate-limited by the promotional cap. A user who has received 5 promotional pushes today can still receive an OTP — the OTP is not a marketing message, and blocking it would be harmful. The limiter therefore has separate buckets: the promotional bucket is hard-capped, the transactional bucket is effectively uncapped (or capped at a very high number to prevent abuse). The category is determined by the notification type, not by the caller — a service cannot mark its promotional message as transactional to bypass the cap.

Quiet hours interaction

A notification deferred by quiet hours still consumes a rate-limit slot when it is sent, not when it is scheduled. This is the correct semantics: the user's experience is the number of notifications received, not the number queued. If the deferred notification is eventually dropped (because the offer expired), the slot is not consumed — the limiter is checked at send time, not at defer time.

User-facing transparency

The cap is a product decision and should be visible: the app's notification settings should show "You can receive up to 2 promotional notifications per day" and let the user set a lower cap. The limiter must honor the user's lower cap (the effective cap is min(system_cap, user_cap)), which means the user preference is consulted before the limiter, and the limiter's cap is the system maximum.

Batching as an alternative to capping

Instead of dropping the third promotional notification, the system can batch: "3 deals for you" is one notification instead of three. Batching respects the user's attention better than a hard cap and delivers more value to the business. The design is to coalesce promotional notifications within a window (e.g., 4 hours) into one, and then apply the cap to the coalesced count. This is more complex than a plain counter but produces a better experience.

The distributed enforcement

Redis is the natural choice because it is fast (sub-millisecond), supports atomic operations, and has TTLs built in. The alternative — a dedicated rate-limit service — adds a network hop but centralizes the policy and makes it easier to change without redeploying every caller. For most systems, a sharded Redis with Lua scripts is sufficient; the rate-limit service is worth it when the policy is complex (multiple tiers, burst allowances, per-segment rules) or when many languages need to enforce it consistently.

Monitoring the limiter

The key metrics: the rate of allowed vs denied per category, the distribution of denials (which users are hitting the cap), and the limiter's latency and error rate. A high denial rate for a category suggests the cap is too low or the campaign is too aggressive; a limiter outage (Redis down) must fail open (allow) or fail closed (deny) — a deliberate choice. Failing open risks spamming users; failing closed risks dropping transactional messages. The standard is fail open for transactional and fail closed for promotional.

Trade-off

A centralized limiter is the only way to enforce a per-user total, but it adds a dependency on the hot path and a failure mode (the limiter is down). A per-service limiter is simpler and more available but does not enforce the user's total. The right answer is a centralized limiter with a local fallback cache that allows a small number of messages when Redis is unavailable, so a limiter outage does not stop all notifications.

8

Push delivery is best-effort. What would you do for a message the user must not miss (e.g., a fraud alert)?

Short Answer

Use an escalation ladder across channels: push, then SMS after a short timeout if not acknowledged, then email, then a voice call for the highest severity. Track delivery acknowledgements per step and advance only when the previous step fails or times out. The in-app inbox is the durable record that the user sees when they next open the app, and it is always written regardless of channel outcome.

The escalation ladder

StepChannelTrigger for next stepTimeout
1Push (APNs/FCM)No delivery receipt or no open30 s
2In-app banner + inboxNo open, user offlineImmediate (parallel)
3SMSNo acknowledgement2 min
4EmailNo acknowledgement10 min
5Voice call (IVR)Highest severity only15 min

The ladder is not a fixed sequence for every alert — it is configured per notification type. A fraud alert might go push → SMS → voice. A password reset might go email → SMS. An order confirmation might go push → in-app only. The severity determines the depth of the ladder and the cost the business is willing to bear (SMS and voice cost money per message).

Delivery acknowledgement

The escalation depends on knowing whether the previous step succeeded. Push is the hardest: APNs and FCM return acceptance, not delivery, and the device may be offline. There are three signals:

  • Provider acceptance: the provider received the request. This is not delivery, but it rules out a provider-side failure.
  • Device receipt: the app acknowledges the notification when it wakes. This is reliable if the app implements it, but it requires background processing that iOS and Android limit.
  • User action: the user opens the notification. This is the strongest signal but also the rarest — many users read the alert on the lock screen without opening the app.

The pragmatic design uses provider acceptance plus a timeout: if the push was accepted and no acknowledgement arrives within 30 seconds, escalate to SMS. The 30-second timeout is a trade-off between false escalations (the user saw the push but did not tap) and delayed escalation (the user is waiting for an SMS that comes late). For fraud alerts, the cost of a false escalation (one extra SMS) is much lower than the cost of a missed alert (a fraudulent transaction), so the timeout is short.

The durable inbox

Regardless of channel outcome, the notification is written to the user's in-app inbox. The inbox is the source of truth: it is available whenever the app opens, it survives device changes, and it is searchable. The push and SMS are prompts to open the app and see the inbox; the inbox itself is the message. This is the "log is the source of truth" principle from Section 1 applied to notifications.

Idempotency across the ladder

A user may receive the push, ignore it, and then receive the SMS. That is two channels for one notification, which is intentional. But the same channel must not send twice: the escalation state machine tracks which channels have been tried and which have been acknowledged, and it never re-sends a channel that already succeeded. The state machine is per notification_id, stored durably, so a restart of the escalation worker resumes where it left off rather than restarting from push.

User control

Escalation must respect the user's preferences for the channels involved. A user who has disabled SMS cannot be escalated to SMS; the ladder must skip that step or fall back to email. The only exception is legally mandated notifications, which may override preferences for the required channel. The user's preference is consulted at each step, not just at the start, because the user may have changed it during the escalation window (rare, but possible).

Cost management

SMS and voice cost real money per message, and an aggressive escalation ladder can become expensive. The design must bound the cost: only high-severity notifications escalate to SMS/voice; the escalation has a maximum depth (e.g., 3 steps); a per-user cost cap prevents a malfunctioning system from sending thousands of SMSes to one user. The cost is monitored per notification type and alerted if it exceeds the budget.

The alternative: signed deep links

For some critical flows, the push itself can be a "tap to act" notification with a signed deep link that takes the user directly to the action (approve transaction, reset password). This is not a delivery guarantee, but it reduces the friction of acting and therefore increases the probability that the user responds to the first step. It is complementary to the escalation ladder, not a replacement.

Common Pitfall

Escalating too aggressively. A user who receives a push, ignores it, and then gets an SMS, an email, and a phone call for a routine notification will uninstall the app. The escalation depth must match the severity — fraud alerts escalate to voice; order confirmations do not. The severity is a property of the notification type, set by the business, not by the sending service.

9

A single event triggers notifications to 50 million users. Where does the design fail first, and how do you fix it?

Short Answer

The design fails first at the provider rate limit, then at the fan-out workers, then at the preference and token lookups. Fifty million notifications in a short window exceeds what any single APNs/FCM connection or email/SMS provider quota can accept. The fix is chunked fan-out with backpressure, multiple provider connections, cached preference and token lookups, and a time-windowed send that spreads the burst.

The arithmetic

50M notifications to push. APNs supports a few thousand requests per second per connection, and FCM has per-project quotas (often tens of thousands per second, but not unlimited). If the campaign must complete in 10 minutes (a "breaking news" alert), the required rate is ~83,000/s. That is at or beyond the single-account provider limit. For email, the limit is the sender reputation and the ESP quota — 50M emails in 10 minutes is 83,000/s, which only large ESPs handle, and only if the sender's reputation is pristine.

The first failure is therefore the provider. The design must either spread the send over a longer window (which conflicts with the "breaking news" urgency) or use multiple provider accounts/connections in parallel.

Failure 2 — the fan-out workers

A single notification service instance cannot process 50M recipients. The fan-out is chunked: the audience is split into chunks of 10,000, each chunk is a task, and 5,000 tasks are distributed across workers. The chunk size is a trade-off: too small means too much task overhead; too large means a slow task blocks a worker. The workers are horizontally scaled, and the queue absorbs the burst. If the workers cannot be scaled fast enough (cold start, quota), the queue grows and the campaign is late.

Failure 3 — the preference and token lookups

For each recipient, the pipeline does a preference lookup, a token lookup, and a rate-limit check. That is 150M Redis operations for a 50M campaign — at 83,000/s, that is 250,000 Redis ops/s. A single Redis node cannot sustain this; a sharded cluster can, but only if the keys are evenly distributed. The fixes: batch the lookups (MGET for 100 users at a time), cache aggressively, and pre-warm the cache before the campaign.

The fixes, in order

  1. Precompute the audience. The segmentation query runs ahead of time and materializes the recipient list. The campaign does not query the database at send time; it reads a precomputed list.
  2. Chunk the fan-out. Split the audience into chunks (10k–50k per chunk), publish each as a task, and let the workers process in parallel. The chunk size determines the granularity of retry and progress tracking.
  3. Batch the lookups. For each chunk, do one MGET for preferences, one for tokens, one pipeline for rate limits. This reduces 3 × N lookups to 3 per chunk.
  4. Spread the send window. If the campaign can complete in 30 minutes instead of 10, the required rate drops by 3×. The scheduler can spread the chunks across the window, with the most important recipients (active users, high-value customers) first.
  5. Multiple provider connections. Use multiple APNs/FCM project credentials or connections to multiply the throughput. This is operational complexity but is the only way to exceed a single account's limit.
  6. Backpressure to the campaign. The campaign's send rate is throttled to the provider's actual capacity, measured in real time. If the provider is slow, the campaign slows; the queue absorbs the difference.
  7. Deduplicate at fan-out. A user in multiple segments must receive one notification, not several. The dedup is by user_id within the campaign, checked before the per-user pipeline runs.

The progress and monitoring

A 50M campaign is a long-running operation that must be observable. The metrics: chunks enqueued, chunks processed, notifications sent, failures, remaining, estimated completion time. If the campaign is falling behind (remaining is not decreasing), the operator can pause it, adjust the rate, or restart with a different configuration. A campaign that is invisible is a campaign that fails silently.

The push payload and content fetch

The push payload is small (title, body, deep link), but the content the user sees after tapping must be served by the app's backend. For a 50M campaign, the tap rate might be 10% — 5M users opening the app within an hour. The backend must handle that spike, either by caching the content at the CDN or by making the destination page static. The notification is only half the load; the destination is the other half.

The alternative: don't fan out

For some use cases, the right answer is not to fan out at all. A "breaking news" alert can be a single push to a topic (APNs/FCM topic subscription) instead of per-device messages — the provider handles the fan-out, and the backend sends one request. This is dramatically cheaper and faster, at the cost of less control (no per-user personalization, no per-user delivery tracking). For broadcast announcements, it is the correct design; for personalized notifications, per-device fan-out is unavoidable.

Trade-off

Faster campaigns require more provider capacity, more workers, and more caching — all of which cost money and add complexity. The right send window is the product requirement: a breaking-news alert justifies the cost of multiple provider connections; a weekly newsletter does not. The design should default to the slower window and only add capacity for campaigns that genuinely need it.

Should notifications support scheduled and future delivery? How would you design that?

Short Answer

Yes — scheduled delivery is required for quiet hours, timezone-aware campaigns, and user-chosen reminder times. The design is a durable scheduler: a table of pending notifications keyed by send_time, a worker that polls due notifications in batches, and a tie-in to the normal pipeline. The scheduler must handle cancellation, rescheduling, timezone changes, and clock skew, and it must scale to millions of pending notifications without scanning the whole table.

Why scheduled delivery is needed

  • Quiet hours. A notification generated at 23:00 local time is deferred to 08:00. That is a scheduled send.
  • Timezone-aware campaigns. A promotional campaign that says "good morning" must send at morning in each user's timezone, not at a single global time.
  • User-chosen reminders. "Remind me tomorrow at 9" is a scheduled notification chosen by the user.
  • Delayed follow-ups. "You left items in your cart" sent 4 hours after the cart was abandoned.
  • Retry after a provider outage. A notification that could not be sent during an outage is rescheduled for when the provider recovers.

The scheduler data model

FieldPurpose
notification_idIdempotency key; unique
user_idRecipient
send_timeWhen to fire (UTC)
payloadThe rendered or renderable notification
statuspending / fired / cancelled / failed
attemptsFiring attempts
created_at, updated_atAudit

The primary key is (send_time, notification_id) or a shard key based on time. The index is on send_time so the worker can query "due notifications" efficiently. The table is sharded by time (e.g., per day) so old entries are dropped as a partition rather than deleted row by row.

The polling worker

A scheduler worker wakes up every few seconds, queries the table for notifications with send_time <= now and status = pending, and enqueues them to the normal pipeline. The query is limited to a batch (e.g., 1,000) and uses the send_time index, so it does not scan the whole table. Multiple workers can run in parallel with a claim mechanism (e.g., UPDATE ... SET status = 'firing' WHERE id IN (...)) to avoid double-firing.

The alternative to polling is a priority queue or a time-wheel, but those are in-memory and lose state on restart. The database is durable, and the polling cost is acceptable if the index is correct. For very high volumes (millions of due notifications per minute), the poll is batched and the workers are scaled horizontally.

Cancellation and rescheduling

A pending notification can be cancelled (the user completed the action, the offer expired) or rescheduled (the user changed the reminder time). The scheduler supports both via an update to the row's status or send_time. The key constraint is that a cancellation must take effect before the worker fires it — a race between the cancel and the fire is possible, and the pipeline must be idempotent (the notification_id check prevents a double-send if the cancel arrives after the fire).

Timezone handling

The scheduler stores send_time in UTC. For timezone-aware delivery, the notification is stored with a local time and the user's timezone, and the worker computes the UTC equivalent. If the user changes timezone (travel), the notification should be rescheduled to the new local time — or left at the original UTC time, depending on the notification type. This is a product decision, and the scheduler must support both (store the timezone alongside the time, and a flag for "follow user timezone").

Clock skew and duplicate firing

Distributed workers have slightly different clocks. A notification scheduled for 09:00:00 might be fired at 08:59:59 by one worker and 09:00:01 by another, if both poll. The claim mechanism (an atomic update to firing status) prevents double-firing: only the worker that successfully claims the row processes it. The claim is idempotent — a second claim on the same row fails, and the second worker skips it. The notification_id dedup in the pipeline is a second line of defence.

Scale

Millions of pending notifications are fine if the table is indexed and sharded by time. The worker's query is bounded by the batch size and the index, so it is O(batch) not O(table). The scheduler's throughput is bounded by the worker count and the database's write capacity for the status updates. For 1M notifications per minute, that is ~17k claims/s — achievable with a sharded database and parallel workers. The table is partitioned by day, so the "due" query only touches today's partition, and old partitions are dropped after their notifications are fired or expired.

The scheduler vs the queue

The scheduler is not a replacement for the queue — it is a front-end to it. The scheduler holds future notifications; when they are due, it enqueues them to the normal pipeline, which handles preferences, rate limits, rendering, and dispatch. The scheduler's only job is timing; everything else is the pipeline's. This separation keeps the scheduler simple and lets the pipeline evolve independently.

Common Pitfall

Scanning the whole table for due notifications. A SELECT * WHERE send_time <= now() without an index on send_time becomes a full scan as the table grows, and the scheduler slows to a crawl. The index and the time-based partitioning are not optional — they are the difference between a scheduler that works at millions of rows and one that works at thousands.
