1

What is event-driven architecture (EDA)? What are its three biggest advantages and three biggest drawbacks compared to synchronous orchestration?

Short Answer

EDA is an architecture where services communicate by producing and consuming events rather than calling each other directly. The advantages are decoupling, resilience, and extensibility. The drawbacks are eventual consistency, debugging complexity, and the operational cost of running a messaging backbone. EDA is not better than RPC — it trades one set of problems for another, and the right choice depends on which problems the system can tolerate.

What EDA actually is

In a synchronous orchestration, service A calls service B, which calls service C, and so on — a chain of RPCs that blocks on each other. The caller knows who the callee is, the callee must be available, and the response flows back through the chain. In EDA, service A publishes an event ("OrderPlaced") to a broker. Service B, C, and D subscribe independently, each processing the event asynchronously. A does not know B, C, or D exist. The event is the contract, and the broker is the channel.

Advantage 1 — Decoupling

A producer does not know its consumers. Adding a new consumer — an analytics service, a fraud detector, a notification service — requires no change to the producer. Removing a consumer likewise. The producer's contract is the event schema, not the set of consumers. This is the biggest organisational advantage: teams can add new reactions to existing events without coordinating with the producer's team. It is also the architectural advantage: the producer's availability does not depend on the consumers', and vice versa.

Advantage 2 — Resilience and load levelling

If a consumer is down, the events accumulate in the broker, and the consumer catches up when it recovers. The producer is not blocked, and the system does not fail because one consumer is slow. This is a direct consequence of the broker's buffering. In a synchronous chain, a slow consumer propagates backpressure to the producer, and a failed consumer fails the whole request. EDA isolates these failures, at the cost of eventual consistency (the consumer's effect is delayed).

Advantage 3 — Extensibility

New business capabilities can be added as new consumers of existing events. A "reward points" service subscribes to "OrderPlaced" and does its thing without touching the order service. This is how organisations scale their architecture with their team structure — a new team adds a new service, not a new integration point in the producer. The event log is the integration surface, and it is additive.

Drawback 1 — Eventual consistency

After "OrderPlaced" is published, the order is created but the inventory is not yet decremented, the email is not yet sent, and the analytics is not yet updated. There is a window where different views of the system disagree. The business must accept this window, and the UI must communicate it ("Order is processing"). In a synchronous chain, the caller waits for all downstream steps, and the response reflects the full state. EDA gives up this immediacy for decoupling and resilience.

The consistency window is not theoretical. It is the sum of the broker's delivery latency, the consumer's processing time, and any retries. Under load, it grows. If a consumer is down for an hour, the window is an hour. The business must design for this — reconciliation jobs, explicit pending states, and user-facing messaging that distinguishes "done" from "in progress".

Drawback 2 — Debugging and reasoning complexity

In a synchronous request, a stack trace shows the full path. In EDA, a business action is a sequence of events across services, with no single call stack. Debugging requires distributed tracing, correlation IDs, and event replay to reconstruct what happened. The system's behaviour is emergent — the outcome of many consumers, each reacting to events in its own way — and reasoning about it requires understanding the whole event graph, not just one service.

This complexity is real and often under-estimated. A team that has only built synchronous systems will find EDA debugging unfamiliar and slower. The tools (tracing, event logging, replay) must be in place before the first incident, not after.

Drawback 3 — Operational cost

The broker is a new distributed system to run: Kafka or RabbitMQ clusters, schema registry, monitoring, retention, replication, upgrades. The consumers are more services to deploy and monitor. The event schemas are contracts that must be versioned. The operational surface grows with the number of producers and consumers, and the on-call rotation must cover the broker as well as the services. For a small team, this cost can exceed the benefits.

When EDA is the wrong choice

  • Small teams: the operational cost of the broker exceeds the decoupling benefit when there are three services and one team.
  • Simple request/response: a login or a price lookup does not benefit from async messaging; the synchronous call is simpler and faster.
  • Strong consistency requirements: if the caller must see all effects before responding, EDA's eventual consistency is unacceptable.
  • Low event volume: a workflow that runs a few times a day does not justify a Kafka cluster; a database queue or a scheduled job is simpler.
Trade-off

EDA trades immediacy and simplicity for decoupling and resilience. The trade is worth it when the system has multiple consumers per event, when the consumers have different availability requirements, or when the organisation needs independent team velocity. It is not worth it when the system is simple, the team is small, or the consistency requirement is strict.

Interview Framing

Say: "EDA's advantages are decoupling, resilience, and extensibility. Its drawbacks are eventual consistency, debugging complexity, and operational cost. I'll use it where multiple consumers need the same event, and keep synchronous RPC for request/response paths that need immediacy." That names both sides and the decision rule.

What is the difference between events and commands? Why does the distinction matter for coupling and failure handling?

Short Answer

A command is an instruction to do something — "charge this card", "send this email". It is addressed to a specific handler and expects the work to be done. An event is a statement of fact — "order 123 was placed". It is broadcast, has no expected handler, and may be consumed by zero or many services. Commands couple the sender to the handler's behaviour; events decouple the producer from everyone who reacts.

AspectCommandEvent
Semantics"Do this""This happened"
TenseImperativePast tense
RecipientsOne handler (or one service)Zero or many consumers
CouplingSender knows what should happenSender knows only that it happened
Failure if unhandledCommand lost — a problemNo harm — it was informational
NamingSendEmail, ChargeCardOrderPlaced, PaymentCaptured
RoutingQueue (point-to-point)Topic (pub/sub)
Delivery guaranteeAt-least-once, with retries until success or DLQAt-least-once, drop if no consumer

Why the distinction matters for coupling

Commands create coupling. If a checkout service sends a SendWelcomeEmail command to an email service, the checkout service now knows that welcome emails exist, and adding a second welcome channel means changing the checkout code. The sender owns the decision that the work should happen, and the recipient is the executor.

Events decouple. If the checkout service publishes UserRegistered, the email service can react without checkout knowing it exists. A new onboarding service can be added without touching checkout. The sender announces a fact and lets the consequences be discovered by the consumers. This is the foundation of extensibility — new reactions do not require changes to the producer.

Why the distinction matters for failure handling

A command that is not handled is a bug. The email was never sent, the payment was never charged. The system must guarantee delivery and retry until it succeeds (or dead-letter). The command's lifecycle is "sent → processed → acknowledged", and the sender tracks the outcome.

An event that is not consumed is fine. No one was listening, no harm done. The event's lifecycle is "published → retained → consumed by whoever cares". There is no sender-side tracking of consumption, because the sender does not know who consumes. The event is a fact; the reactions are the consumers' business.

The naming test

The easiest way to tell them apart is grammatical. If the name is imperative ("SendEmail", "ResizeImage", "ChargeCard"), it is a command. If it is past tense and describes a fact ("OrderPlaced", "ImageUploaded", "PaymentCaptured"), it is an event. The name reflects the contract: a command implies a handler and an expected effect; an event implies an announcement and open-ended reactions.

When commands are correct

Commands are not always wrong. When the sender genuinely needs the work done by a specific service, and the contract is intentional, a command is the right primitive. "ChargeCard" is a command to a specific payment service; it should not be broadcast to everyone. The rule is: use commands when the sender owns the decision and the recipient is known; use events when the sender is announcing a fact and the reactions are open-ended.

The two can coexist in one system. The order service issues a "ReserveInventory" command to the inventory service (because the order cannot be placed without the reservation), and then publishes "OrderPlaced" (a fact that many services can react to). The command is the synchronous part of the workflow; the event is the broadcast that allows other services to react asynchronously.

The outbox interaction

Both commands and events need the same delivery guarantee: no loss. The transactional outbox (Question 5) is the mechanism for both — the command or event is written to the outbox in the same transaction as the business change, and a relay publishes it. The distinction is in the consumer's contract, not in the delivery mechanism. The outbox is agnostic to whether the message is a command or an event.

Common Pitfall

Calling a command an event to sound modern. SendWelcomeEmail is not an event, no matter what topic it is on. The sender still expects the email to be sent, and the system still needs to guarantee that. The naming confusion leads to the wrong delivery guarantees and the wrong failure handling — the "event" is dropped when no consumer is listening, but the sender assumed it was a command.

Interview Framing

Say: "Commands are imperative and addressed; events are past tense and broadcast. Commands couple the sender to a handler and require delivery guarantees; events decouple and can be dropped if no one listens. I'll use commands for the synchronous parts of the workflow and events for the broadcast reactions." That names the distinction and the design choice.

What is event sourcing? How does it differ from state-based persistence, and what role does a message log play?

Short Answer

Event sourcing stores the sequence of events that produced an entity's state, rather than the current state itself. The state is derived by replaying the events. A message log (Kafka, an event store) is the natural storage for the event sequence — append-only, ordered, and replayable. The trade-off is reconstructing state on every read versus storing it directly, and the benefits are a full audit trail, the ability to rebuild any past state, and a natural fit with event-driven consumers.

State-based persistence (the default)

Most systems store the current state of an entity. An orders table holds one row per order with its current status, total, and timestamps. When the order is placed, the row is inserted; when it ships, the status is updated; when it is refunded, another update. The history of changes is overwritten — the row shows the current state, not how it got there.

This is simple and fast: reading an order is a single row lookup. The history is lost unless a separate audit table is maintained. The state is the source of truth, and any derived view (a report, a search index) is built from it.

Event sourcing

Event sourcing stores the events, not the state. An order_events log holds every event for every order: OrderPlaced, PaymentReceived, OrderShipped, OrderDelivered. The current state of an order is computed by replaying its events in order. The event log is the source of truth; the state is a derived view.

To get the current state, you replay the events. To get the state at a past time, you replay up to that time. To audit how the state changed, you read the events. To add a new derived view (a search index, a dashboard, a recommendation), you replay the events into the new view. The event log is immutable and append-only; nothing is ever updated or deleted.

The comparison

The role of the message log

Kafka is a natural fit for event sourcing because it is an ordered, append-only, replayable log. Each entity's events are written to a partition keyed by the entity ID, so the events for one entity are ordered. Consumers read the log and build materialized views — a current-state table, a search index, a report. The log is the source of truth; the views are derived and can be rebuilt by replaying the log.

The log's retention is the constraint: if the log is deleted after 7 days, the events are gone, and the views cannot be rebuilt from scratch. For true event sourcing, the log must be retained indefinitely (or snapshotted periodically so the old events can be dropped). Kafka's log compaction can retain the latest event per entity (enough to rebuild the current state) but loses the full history; a separate, non-compacted topic is needed for the full audit.

Snapshots

Replaying thousands of events to reconstruct one entity's state is slow. The standard optimization is snapshots: every N events, store the current state as a snapshot. To reconstruct, load the latest snapshot and replay the events after it. The snapshot is a cache of the replay; the events remain the source of truth.

Snapshots are not required for correctness — replaying from the beginning always works — but they are required for performance at scale. The snapshot interval (every 100 events, every 1,000 events) is a trade-off between snapshot storage and replay time.

When event sourcing is the right choice

  • Audit is required: financial transactions, healthcare records, compliance — the full history must be preserved.
  • Temporal queries: "what was the order's state at 3 PM yesterday?" requires the events, not the current state.
  • Multiple read models: the same events feed a current-state view, a search index, a dashboard, and a recommendation engine. Each is built by replaying the events.
  • Event-driven integration: other services consume the same events, so the event log is both the source of truth and the integration channel.
  • Complex domain with many state transitions: the events capture the domain's behaviour more naturally than a state machine table.

When event sourcing is the wrong choice

  • Simple CRUD: a settings page or a product catalogue does not need event sourcing; a state table is simpler and faster.
  • No history requirement: if the business does not need the audit trail, the storage cost and complexity are unjustified.
  • Read-heavy with no derived views: if the only read is the current state, the replay cost is pure overhead.
  • Small team: event sourcing adds complexity (schema evolution, snapshots, view rebuilds) that a small team may not sustain.
Trade-off

Event sourcing gives a complete audit trail and the ability to rebuild any view, at the cost of storage (one row per event, not per entity), read latency (replay or materialized view), and complexity (snapshots, schema evolution, view maintenance). It is the right choice when the history matters, and the wrong choice when it does not.

Interview Framing

Say: "Event sourcing stores the events, not the state. The state is derived by replaying, or by a materialized view that is kept up to date. Kafka is the natural log for the events. I'd use it for the ledger because audit is required, and not for the product catalogue because it is simple CRUD." That names the mechanism, the storage, and the decision rule.

What is CQRS? How does a message queue or event stream enable separate read and write models?

Short Answer

CQRS (Command Query Responsibility Segregation) separates the write model from the read model. The write model handles commands and produces events; the read model consumes the events and builds a query-optimized view. The message queue is the bridge: it decouples the write path from the read path, lets each scale independently, and lets the read model be rebuilt by replaying the events.

Why separate read and write models

Reads and writes have different shapes. A write is a transaction: validate the command, check invariants, persist the change. A read is a query: fetch the data in the shape the UI needs, often joined and aggregated across entities. In a single model, the schema must serve both, and one of them suffers. A normalized schema is good for writes (no duplication, clear invariants) and bad for reads (joins, aggregates). A denormalized schema is good for reads and bad for writes (update anomalies, complex consistency).

CQRS separates them: the write model is normalized and transactional; the read model is denormalized and query-shaped. Each is optimized for its purpose, and the two are kept in sync by the event stream.

How the queue enables it

  1. Write: the command handler validates the command, writes to the write model (the normalized database), and publishes an event to the stream. The write is complete when the event is published (or in the outbox).
  2. Bridge: the event stream carries the change to the read side. The stream is the decoupling point: the write model does not know which read models exist, and the read models do not know about each other.
  3. Read: the read model consumer applies the event to its own store — a denormalized table, a search index, a materialized view. The read is served from this store, which is shaped for the UI's query.

The queue's role is to be the durable, ordered channel between the two. It provides:

  • Decoupling: the write model and read models are independent services; the stream is the contract.
  • Ordering: events are delivered in order (per key), so the read model applies them correctly.
  • Replay: a new read model can be built by replaying the event history; a broken one can be rebuilt.
  • Multiple read models: several consumers can read the same stream, each building a different view.
  • Buffering: if a read model is slow or down, the stream buffers the events; the read model catches up when it recovers.

The read model

The read model is a denormalized view shaped for a specific query. Three common forms:

FormStorageUse case
Materialized tableSQL table with denormalized columnsFast point lookups, dashboards
Search indexElasticsearch / OpenSearchFull-text search, filters
Aggregate/counter tableKey-value store (Redis, DynamoDB)Leaderboards, counts, summaries

Each read model is a consumer of the event stream, and each is maintained independently. A new read model (a new dashboard, a new search feature) is added without touching the write model or the other read models. This is the extensibility CQRS provides.

The consistency window

The read model lags the write model by the stream's delivery latency plus the consumer's processing time. The write is complete when the event is published, but the read model may not reflect it for milliseconds to seconds. A client that writes and immediately reads may see the old value — the read-your-writes problem, applied at the read model level.

The standard mitigations:

  • Read your own writes: route the user's read to the write model (or to a read model known to be caught up) for a short window after the write.
  • Version token: the write returns a version; the read waits for the read model to reach that version before serving.
  • Optimistic UI: the client shows the expected result before the read model confirms; reconciles when the read model catches up.
  • Accept the lag: for read models that are not user-facing (analytics, search), the lag is acceptable.

CQRS and event sourcing

CQRS and event sourcing are related but distinct. Event sourcing is about the write model's storage (events, not state). CQRS is about separating read and write paths. They are often used together — an event-sourced write model naturally produces the events that feed the read models — but each can be used without the other:

  • Event sourcing without CQRS: the events are the storage, and reads replay them. No separate read model.
  • CQRS without event sourcing: the write model is a normal database; the events are published via CDC or outbox. The read models are separate.
  • Both: the write model stores events, and the read models are built by replaying. This is the most common combination.

When CQRS is the right choice

  • Read and write workloads differ: the reads are complex queries and the writes are simple transactions; separate models optimize each.
  • Multiple read shapes: the same data is read by a web UI, a mobile API, a search, and an analytics dashboard — each needs a different shape.
  • Event-driven integration: other services consume the events; the read model is one more consumer.
  • Event sourcing is in use: the events are already the source of truth; read models are the natural derived views.

When CQRS is the wrong choice

  • Simple CRUD: one model serves both reads and writes; CQRS adds complexity for no benefit.
  • Strong consistency required: the eventual consistency of the read model is unacceptable for the UI.
  • Small team: two models, two stores, and the sync between them is a lot of code and operations.
Common Pitfall

Building CQRS for every entity because it is "the pattern". CQRS is a per-entity decision: the order aggregate may need it (complex read shapes, event-driven), the settings entity does not (single read, single write). Applying it uniformly adds complexity to the entities that do not benefit.

Interview Framing

Say: "CQRS separates the write model (normalized, transactional) from the read model (denormalized, query-shaped). The event stream is the bridge, and the read model is rebuilt by replaying. I'll use it for the order view because the write is transactional and the read has multiple shapes, and not for the settings page because it is simple CRUD." That names the pattern, the bridge, and the decision rule.

Part B — Contracts & Coordination Outbox, schemas, and sagas

How do you ensure consistency when a business transaction spans a database write and a message publish? Explain the transactional outbox pattern.

Short Answer

A database write and a message publish cannot be atomic across two systems. The transactional outbox solves it: the message is written to an outbox table in the same database transaction as the business change, and a separate relay process reads the outbox and publishes to the queue. The business change and the message are atomic (same transaction); the publish is eventually consistent (relay retries until it succeeds).

The problem

A service places an order: it must write the order to the database and publish "OrderPlaced" to Kafka. The two writes are in different systems, and there is no distributed transaction covering both. The possible failures:

  • DB write succeeds, publish fails: the order exists but no one is notified. The inventory is not decremented, the email is not sent, the analytics is not updated. The order is invisible to the rest of the system.
  • Publish succeeds, DB write fails: the event is published but the order does not exist. Consumers react to a phantom order, and the downstream effects cannot be reconciled.

Retrying the operation does not help: a retry may duplicate the successful step. The problem is the lack of atomicity across two systems.

The outbox solution

  1. Create an outbox table in the same database as the business tables. Columns: id, aggregate_id, event_type, payload, created_at, published_at (nullable).
  2. Write the business change and the outbox row in one transaction. The order INSERT and the outbox INSERT are committed together or not at all.
  3. A relay process reads the outbox for unpublished rows, publishes each to the queue, and marks it published. The relay retries on failure; the outbox row remains unpublished until the publish succeeds.
  4. The consumer receives the event and processes it idempotently (dedup by outbox ID or event ID).

Why it works

The atomicity of the business change and the outbox row is provided by the database's local transaction, which is well-understood and reliable. The publish is a separate step, but it is retried until it succeeds, so the event is guaranteed to be published eventually. The consumer sees the event after the business change is committed, so there is no phantom order. The overall guarantee is exactly-once effects: the business change happens once, the event is published once (or deduplicated if published twice), and the consumer processes it once (or deduplicates).

The relay

The relay is a separate process (or a thread within the service) that polls the outbox. The polling frequency is a trade-off: frequent polling reduces latency but adds database load; infrequent polling reduces load but increases latency. The standard is 100–500 ms polling, with batching (read 100 outbox rows per poll). For lower latency, CDC (e.g., Debezium reading the database's log) can replace polling — the relay becomes a log consumer instead of a poller.

The relay must be idempotent: publishing the same outbox row twice is harmless if the consumer deduplicates by outbox ID. The relay marks the row as published only after the publish succeeds; if it crashes between the publish and the mark, the next poll republishes the row (a duplicate the consumer must handle).

The polling vs CDC choice

ApproachLatencyDB loadComplexity
PollingPolling interval (100–500 ms)Periodic queries, even when emptySimple — a query and a publish
CDC (Debezium)Near-real-time (ms)Reads the DB log; minimal query loadHigher — a CDC pipeline to run
Trigger-basedImmediate (on commit)Trigger overhead on every insertMedium — database-specific

Polling is the default for its simplicity. CDC is better at high volume or low latency. Trigger-based is rarely used because the trigger's side effect (publishing) is not transactional anyway.

The outbox vs 2PC

Two-phase commit could, in theory, make the DB write and the publish atomic. But 2PC requires the message broker to support the protocol (Kafka does not), it is blocking (a coordinator failure holds locks), and it is slow (extra round trips). The outbox avoids all three: the local transaction is fast and non-blocking, and the relay is eventually consistent. This is why the outbox is the standard, and 2PC across DB and queue is almost never used.

The outbox at the architecture level

The outbox is not just a per-service pattern — it is an architectural primitive. Every service that writes to its database and publishes an event uses an outbox. The relay is a shared component (or a per-service thread). The event stream is the integration bus, and the outbox is the bridge from the database to the bus. This is the mechanism that makes "database per service" compatible with "event-driven integration" without distributed transactions.

The cleanup

The outbox grows as rows accumulate. Published rows can be deleted after a retention period (e.g., 7 days), either by the relay or by a separate cleanup job. The retention is long enough to cover any plausible re-publish (if a consumer needs to replay from the outbox, unlikely) and short enough to keep the table small. The table is partitioned by time and old partitions are dropped.

Trade-off

The outbox gives exactly-once effects across the DB-queue boundary at the cost of a relay process and slight latency (polling interval or CDC lag). The alternative — dual-write or 2PC — is either incorrect (dual-write can diverge) or operationally heavy (2PC is blocking and rarely supported). The outbox is the pragmatic best.

Interview Framing

Say: "The DB write and the message publish can't be atomic. I'll use the transactional outbox: write the business row and the outbox row in one transaction, and a relay publishes the outbox. The publish is eventually consistent, and the consumer deduplicates by outbox ID, so the effect is exactly-once." That names the mechanism, the guarantee, and the consumer's responsibility.

How do you design an event schema that can evolve without breaking consumers? What is a schema registry, and why is compatibility important?

Short Answer

Event schemas must evolve as the domain changes, and the evolution must be compatible with existing consumers. A schema registry stores versioned schemas and enforces compatibility rules (backward, forward, full) at publish time. The producer evolves the schema under the rule, and the consumers can upgrade at their own pace without breaking.

Why schemas matter

An event is a contract between producer and consumer. The consumer parses the event's fields, and if the producer removes or renames a field, the consumer breaks. In a synchronous API, the break is immediate and visible (the response is wrong). In EDA, the break is delayed and silent: the consumer processes a malformed event, fails in an unexpected way, and the failure appears far from the schema change.

The schema must be explicit (not inferred from JSON) so that the contract is documented and enforced. Avro, Protobuf, and JSON Schema are the common formats. Avro is the most common in Kafka for its compactness and schema-evolution support; Protobuf is common in gRPC-heavy environments; JSON Schema is readable but verbose.

Compatibility modes

ModeRuleAllowsBreaks
Backward New schema can read old data Adding optional fields, removing required fields (with defaults) Consumers running the new schema on old data
Forward Old schema can read new data Adding required fields with defaults, removing optional fields Consumers running the old schema on new data
Full Both backward and forward Adding optional, removing optional, renaming with aliases Anything that violates either direction
None No compatibility check Anything Every consumer, eventually

The right mode depends on the upgrade order. If consumers are upgraded first (they can read the new schema), the producer can adopt a backward-compatible change. If producers are upgraded first (they emit the new schema before consumers can read it), the change must be forward-compatible. In practice, full compatibility is the safest default — it allows the producer and consumers to upgrade in any order.

The schema registry

A schema registry is a service that stores the schemas (keyed by subject and version), assigns schema IDs, and enforces compatibility at registration. The producer, when it publishes an event, registers the schema (if new) and includes the schema ID in the message header. The consumer looks up the schema by ID and deserializes accordingly.

The registry is the source of truth for the contract. It prevents a producer from publishing an incompatible schema — the registration fails, and the producer is forced to evolve the schema under the rule. This is the mechanism that makes compatibility enforceable, not just advisory.

Common compatible changes

  • Add an optional field with a default: backward and forward compatible (consumers that do not know the field ignore it; consumers that know it use the default when absent).
  • Remove an optional field: full compatible (consumers that know it use the default; consumers that do not know it ignore the removal).
  • Rename a field with an alias: Avro supports aliases, so the old name and the new name are both valid; consumers using the old name still work.
  • Widen a type: int → long, float → double — allowed in Avro and Protobuf.
  • Add a new enum value: compatible if the consumer handles unknown values (Avro allows this with a default).

Common incompatible changes

  • Remove a required field: old data does not have it, and new consumers cannot parse. Incompatible.
  • Add a required field without a default: old producers do not emit it, and new consumers fail. Incompatible unless forward-only.
  • Rename a field without an alias: consumers using the old name break. Incompatible.
  • Change a field's type incompatibly: string → int, or a struct's shape. Incompatible.
  • Change the meaning of a field: the type is the same, but the semantics differ. Incompatible in practice, even if the registry says it is compatible.

The versioning strategy

Two common strategies:

  • Single topic, multiple schema versions: the topic carries events with different schema versions, distinguished by the schema ID in the header. Consumers handle all versions. This is the registry's native mode and the most common.
  • New topic per major version: a breaking change creates a new topic (e.g., orders.v2) and the old topic is deprecated. This is simpler for consumers but requires migration of producers and consumers, and it loses the continuity of the topic's history.

The first is preferable for compatible changes (add field, remove optional), the second for genuinely breaking changes (new event shape, different semantics).

The consumer's responsibility

Even with a registry and full compatibility, the consumer must be robust to schema changes: ignore unknown fields (Avro and Protobuf do this natively), handle missing optional fields with defaults, and log schema mismatches. A consumer that assumes every field is present will break on the first optional field addition — even though the schema is compatible.

Common Pitfall

Using JSON without a schema and relying on "it's just JSON, it's flexible". JSON is flexible, but without a schema there is no contract, no compatibility check, and no way to detect a breaking change until a consumer fails in production. The registry plus an explicit schema is what makes evolution safe.

What is the difference between choreography and orchestration for sagas? When would you choose each?

Short Answer

A saga is a distributed transaction implemented as a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions. Choreography lets each service react to events from the previous service — no central coordinator. Orchestration uses a central orchestrator that tells each service what to do. Choreography is more decoupled but harder to reason about; orchestration is easier to debug but introduces a central component.

DimensionChoreographyOrchestration
Control flowDecentralized — each service reacts to eventsCentralized — one orchestrator drives
CouplingLoose — services know only the eventsTighter — services are called by the orchestrator
VisibilityHard — the flow is spread across servicesEasy — the orchestrator has the full state
Failure handlingEach service must handle its own compensationThe orchestrator handles compensation
New stepAdd a new consumerModify the orchestrator
ComplexityGrows with the number of participantsConcentrated in the orchestrator
Best forSimple, linear flows with few participantsComplex flows with many participants or branching

Choreography in detail

The saga is a chain of events. The order service places an order and publishes OrderPlaced. The payment service consumes it, charges the card, and publishes PaymentCaptured. The inventory service consumes it, reserves the stock, and publishes InventoryReserved. The shipping service consumes it, creates the shipment, and publishes ShipmentCreated. If any step fails, the service publishes a failure event, and the previous services consume it and compensate (refund the card, release the stock).

There is no central coordinator. Each service knows the events it consumes and the events it produces. The flow is implicit in the event graph. This is the most decoupled form — adding a new step is a new consumer, and no existing service changes.

The problem is visibility. The saga's state is spread across the participants' databases and the events between them. To know whether a saga succeeded, you must trace the events. To debug a stuck saga, you must reconstruct the chain. When the number of participants grows, the graph becomes hard to reason about, and the compensation logic is scattered.

Orchestration in detail

A central orchestrator (a saga coordinator) drives the flow. It calls the order service to place the order, the payment service to charge the card, the inventory service to reserve stock, and the shipping service to create the shipment. Each call returns a result (success or failure), and the orchestrator decides the next step. If a step fails, the orchestrator calls the compensating actions for the previous steps.

The orchestrator has the full state of the saga: which steps succeeded, which failed, which compensations ran. Debugging is a matter of looking at the orchestrator's state, not reconstructing from events. The compensation logic is centralized — the orchestrator knows which compensation to call for each failure.

The problem is coupling. The orchestrator knows every service and every step. Adding a new step means modifying the orchestrator. The orchestrator is a new component to build, deploy, and monitor, and it must be highly available (if it fails, the sagas are stuck). It can become a bottleneck if it handles many sagas.

When to choose each

  • Choreography: the saga is linear (a chain of steps), the participants are few (2–5), the compensation is simple (one compensating action per step), and the team values decoupling over visibility.
  • Orchestration: the saga is complex (branching, parallel steps, conditional logic), the participants are many (5+), the compensation is complex (multiple compensation steps per failure), or the team needs operational visibility (a dashboard of in-flight sagas).

Most teams start with choreography (simpler for the first flow) and move to orchestration when the complexity grows. The migration is not trivial, but it is the natural progression.

The hybrid

In practice, a system can use both: choreography for the simple, linear flows, and orchestration for the complex, multi-step flows. The choice is per-saga, not per-system. A team with a saga orchestrator can still use choreography for the simple flows that do not justify the orchestrator's overhead.

The orchestrator's design

The orchestrator is a state machine. It persists the saga's state (which step, what was the result, what compensations ran) in a database. It calls each service via a command (Section 6's command, not an event), waits for the result (synchronously or by consuming a reply event), and advances the state machine. The orchestrator's state is the saga's source of truth.

The orchestrator must be idempotent: a retry of a step must not duplicate its effect. It must handle timeouts (a step that does not respond within a window is treated as failed). And it must be durable (a crashed orchestrator must resume from the persisted state, not from the beginning). Temporal, Cadence, and AWS Step Functions are managed orchestrators that provide these properties.

Common Pitfall

Choosing choreography for a complex saga because it is "more decoupled". A 10-step saga with branching and compensation is unmanageable as a choreography — the event graph is too complex, the compensation is scattered, and debugging is a nightmare. Orchestration is the right choice when the saga has non-trivial logic.

Interview Framing

Say: "Choreography for simple linear flows — order, payment, inventory — because it is decoupled and each service knows only its events. Orchestration for complex flows with branching or many steps, because the orchestrator has the state and the compensation logic. Most systems start with choreography and move to orchestration as complexity grows." That names both and the decision rule.

How do you integrate a message queue into a system that already uses synchronous RPC? What are the failure modes of mixing both?

Short Answer

Integrate at the boundaries: keep synchronous RPC for user-facing request/response, and use messaging for cross-service notifications and long-running work. The bridge is the outbox on the write side and a synchronous acknowledgment on the read side. The failure modes come from mixing the two — partial failures that are neither fully sync nor fully async, timeouts that leave the caller uncertain, and consistency windows that the UI does not communicate.

The integration pattern

  1. Synchronous entry: the user's request hits the API, which validates and calls the core service synchronously.
  2. Write + outbox: the core service writes the business change and the outbox row in one transaction. The synchronous call returns success.
  3. Relay publishes: the outbox relay publishes the event to the queue. The downstream services consume asynchronously.
  4. Optional synchronous confirmation: if the user needs to know the downstream result (e.g., the payment succeeded), the API can poll a status endpoint or subscribe to a reply channel, with a timeout. If the timeout expires, the API returns "processing" and the client polls.
  5. Asynchronous updates: the user's client receives updates via WebSocket (Section 5) or push (Section 4) as the downstream completes.

The pattern is "synchronous for the user's request, asynchronous for the downstream work". The user gets an immediate response (the order was accepted), and the downstream effects complete asynchronously with the user being notified when they do.

Failure mode 1 — The mixed transaction

A service tries to be both sync and async: it writes to the database, calls a downstream service synchronously, and publishes an event. If the sync call succeeds but the publish fails, the system is inconsistent — the downstream was called but the event was not published. If the sync call fails but the publish succeeds, the event says the work was done but it was not.

The fix is to pick one model per operation: either the operation is fully synchronous (the caller waits for all effects) or fully asynchronous (the caller gets an ack and the effects complete later). Mixing them in one operation creates exactly the inconsistency the outbox is meant to prevent.

Failure mode 2 — The unknown-timeout

The API calls a service synchronously, the call times out, and the API does not know whether the service processed the request. This is the classic ambiguity of synchronous RPC. In an async system, the timeout is not a failure — the caller gets an ack immediately, and the downstream completes later. In a mixed system, the caller faces the ambiguity and must either retry (risking a duplicate) or return an error (risking a lost request). The fix is idempotency on the downstream (Section 6) so retries are safe.

Failure mode 3 — The consistency window not communicated

The user's synchronous call returns success, but the downstream effects are asynchronous. The user immediately reads the data and does not see the downstream effect (the order is placed but the shipping is not yet scheduled). If the UI shows "done", the user is confused. The fix is to communicate the state: "Order placed, shipping will be scheduled shortly", with a status that updates when the downstream completes.

Failure mode 4 — The synchronous bottleneck

A service that is mostly async but has one synchronous call in the middle becomes coupled to that call's latency and availability. If the downstream is slow, the upstream is slow; if it is down, the upstream is down. The async parts were supposed to decouple, but the sync call reintroduces the coupling. The fix is to make the sync call async too, or to make it optional (degrade gracefully if the downstream is unavailable).

Failure mode 5 — The dual-write

The service writes to the database and publishes to the queue without the outbox. This is the dual-write problem — one can succeed and the other fail, and the system diverges. The fix is the outbox (Question 5). Every service that writes and publishes must use it; a service that uses the outbox in one place and dual-writes in another has an inconsistency waiting to happen.

Failure mode 6 — The retry storm

The synchronous caller retries on timeout; the async consumer retries on failure. A slow downstream triggers both, multiplying the load. The fix is a retry budget (Section 4) and a circuit breaker on the sync call, so the retries are bounded and the slow downstream is protected.

The migration strategy

For a system that is already synchronous, the migration to async is incremental:

  1. Start with the least latency-sensitive flows: notifications, analytics, audit logs. These can be made async without affecting the user.
  2. Add the outbox to the write path: every write publishes an event, even if no consumer exists yet. This makes future consumers trivial to add.
  3. Move cross-team integrations to events: the team-to-team dependencies are the biggest coupling; making them async is the biggest win.
  4. Keep user-facing request/response synchronous: the user still needs an immediate answer; the async parts are the downstream effects.
  5. Add the status/notification layer: the user learns about the async completion via WebSocket, push, or polling.

The end state is a hybrid: synchronous for the user's request, asynchronous for the downstream work, with the outbox as the bridge. This is how most production systems are built, and the "pure" EDA (everything async) is rare because users want immediate answers.

Trade-off

Mixing sync and async gives the best of both — immediate user responses and decoupled downstream work — at the cost of two failure models to reason about, two sets of retry logic, and the consistency window that must be communicated. The alternative (all sync) is simpler to reason about but couples the system to every downstream's latency; the alternative (all async) is decoupled but gives the user no immediate answer. The hybrid is the pragmatic choice.

How do you decide between synchronous and asynchronous communication for a given boundary?

Short Answer

Ask four questions about the interaction: does the caller need the response to continue? can the work be done later without harming the user? does the caller care about the outcome or just that it happened? and how many consumers need the result? Synchronous when the answer is "yes, immediate"; asynchronous when the work can be deferred and the caller does not need to wait.

The four questions

QuestionSyncAsync
Does the caller need the response to continue?Yes — the next step depends on itNo — the caller can proceed
Can the work be deferred?No — the user is waitingYes — seconds or minutes are fine
Does the caller care about the outcome?Yes — success or failure changes the flowNo — the fact that it happened is enough
How many consumers need it?One — a specific handlerMany — independent reactions

If the interaction is "the user clicked a button and needs the result", it is synchronous. If it is "the system should notify other services that something happened", it is asynchronous. Most boundaries are obvious; the difficult ones are where the caller needs a result but the work is slow.

The synchronous cases

  • Authentication: the login request must verify the credentials before returning the token. Synchronous.
  • Price lookup: the checkout page needs the current price to render. Synchronous (with caching).
  • Payment authorization: the checkout must know whether the charge succeeded before confirming the order. Synchronous (with a timeout and a fallback).
  • Read queries: the user requests data; the response is the data. Synchronous by nature.
  • Lock acquisition: the process must know whether it has the lock before proceeding. Synchronous.

The asynchronous cases

  • Notifications: the order was placed; the email can go out in a second. Async.
  • Analytics: the page view should be recorded; the response does not depend on it. Async.
  • Image processing: the upload was accepted; the thumbnail can be generated in a minute. Async.
  • Cross-service notifications: the order was placed; inventory, shipping, and billing should react independently. Async (pub/sub).
  • Long workflows: the refund should be processed; it takes minutes and involves several steps. Async (saga).

The hard cases

Some boundaries need both. The checkout is the canonical example:

  • The payment authorization is synchronous — the user must know the charge succeeded before the order is confirmed.
  • The order confirmation email is asynchronous — the user does not need to wait for it.
  • The inventory reservation is synchronous if the checkout must fail on insufficient stock (the user sees "out of stock" immediately), or asynchronous if the order is accepted and the failure is handled later (the user sees "order placed" and the reservation runs in the background).
  • The shipment scheduling is asynchronous — the user does not need to know until the shipment is created.

The checkout is a hybrid: synchronous for the critical path (payment, inventory), asynchronous for the side effects (email, shipment, analytics). The boundary is not the whole flow — it is per step, chosen by the four questions.

The failure and latency constraints

Two additional factors weigh on the decision:

  • Failure isolation: if the downstream is unavailable, a synchronous call fails the whole request; an asynchronous call is buffered and retried. If the downstream's availability is independent of the caller's, async isolates the failure.
  • Latency budget: a synchronous call adds its latency to the request. If the budget is tight (e.g., a 200 ms page load), a 100 ms downstream call is a quarter of the budget. Async moves the latency off the critical path.

The default

When in doubt, ask: "if this step fails, does the user need to know immediately?" If yes, synchronous. If no, asynchronous. Most steps are "no" — the user does not need to know that the email was sent, the analytics was recorded, or the thumbnail was generated. Those should be async. The few steps that are "yes" — payment, inventory, authentication — should be synchronous, with the other steps moved off the critical path.

The other default: "if the interaction has more than one consumer, it is an event, not a call". A synchronous call is point-to-point; if multiple services need to know, they should be subscribers to an event, not callees of a chain.

Interview Framing

Say: "I'll make the payment synchronous because the user needs the result, and the email, analytics, and shipment async because they can happen after the response. The rule is: does the user need to know immediately? If yes, sync; if no, async." That names the decision criterion and applies it per step.

Capstone: design an event-driven order processing system from checkout to delivery.

Short Answer

The order service is the write model: it accepts the checkout request, validates, writes the order and outbox in one transaction, and returns an ack. The outbox publishes OrderPlaced to the event stream. Payment, inventory, shipping, and notification each consume the event independently and publish their own events. The read model is a denormalized order view maintained by a consumer. The payment step is synchronous (the user needs the result); everything else is asynchronous. The saga is choreographed for simplicity, with an orchestrator added if the flow grows complex.

The requirements

  • Accept an order from the user and confirm the payment within the request.
  • Reserve inventory so the order can be fulfilled.
  • Schedule shipment once the inventory is reserved.
  • Notify the user at each stage (confirmation, shipment, delivery).
  • Provide a consistent view of the order's current state to the user and to support.
  • Handle failures with compensation (refund if inventory is unavailable, etc.).
  • Be auditable — every step is recorded.

The architecture

The checkout flow

  1. User submits order to the API. The API validates the request (fields, user, cart) and calls the order service synchronously.
  2. Order service writes the order row and the OrderPlaced outbox row in one transaction. Returns the order ID to the API.
  3. API calls the payment service synchronously (within the same request) to charge the card. Payment service calls the provider with an idempotency key, writes the charge result and the PaymentCaptured outbox row in one transaction.
  4. API returns success to the user: "Order 123 placed, payment confirmed."
  5. Outbox relays publish OrderPlaced and PaymentCaptured to the stream.
  6. Inventory service consumes PaymentCaptured, reserves the stock, publishes InventoryReserved (or InventoryFailed).
  7. Shipping service consumes InventoryReserved, creates the shipment, publishes ShipmentCreated.
  8. Notification service consumes each event and sends the appropriate notification (confirmation, shipment, delivery).
  9. Order view consumes each event and updates the denormalized order state (status, timestamps, tracking number).

Why payment is synchronous and the rest is async

The user needs to know whether the payment succeeded before the checkout is complete. A payment failure must be visible immediately ("Your card was declined"). The payment is the critical path, and making it async would mean the user sees "order placed" and then "payment failed" later — a poor experience, and a wasted order if the payment fails.

Inventory reservation is arguably also critical (out-of-stock should fail the order), but it is often made async for scalability — the order is accepted, and the reservation runs in the background. The trade-off: the user is told "order placed" even if the item is out of stock, and the failure is handled later (refund, notify). This is a product decision. In the capstone, payment is synchronous (must succeed for the order to be confirmed), inventory is asynchronous (the order is accepted, and the reservation runs; if it fails, the order is cancelled and the payment refunded).

The compensation flow

If inventory fails after payment succeeded, the saga compensates:

  1. Inventory publishes InventoryFailed.
  2. The order service consumes it, marks the order as cancelled, and publishes OrderCancelled.
  3. The payment service consumes OrderCancelled, refunds the charge (with an idempotency key), and publishes RefundProcessed.
  4. The notification service sends "Your order was cancelled and refunded".
  5. The order view reflects the cancelled state.

The compensation is choreographed: each service reacts to the failure event and does its part. If the flow grows complex (partial shipments, split payments, multiple compensation paths), an orchestrator is added to drive it explicitly.

The read model

The user's order page reads from the order view — a denormalized table with the current status, shipment tracking, and timestamps, all updated by consuming events. The view is a CQRS read model: the write model (order service) is normalized and transactional; the read model (order view) is shaped for the UI's query. The view is rebuilt by replaying the events if needed.

The read model lags the write model by the event latency. The user's own order page can be served from the write model (read-your-writes) for a short window after checkout, and from the read model after. Or the checkout response already includes the order's state, and the client uses that as the initial view.

What the design gets right

  • Synchronous for the user's critical path: payment is confirmed before the order is accepted, so the user knows immediately.
  • Asynchronous for the downstream: inventory, shipping, notification, and the read model are decoupled, so a slow or down service does not affect checkout.
  • Exactly-once effects: the outbox (DB-queue boundary), the provider idempotency key (external call), and the consumer dedup (event processing) make every step safe to retry.
  • Auditable: the outbox and the event stream are the audit trail; every step is recorded.
  • Extensible: a new consumer (loyalty points, fraud detection, analytics) subscribes to the existing events without touching the producers.
  • Consistent read model: the order view is a purpose-built read model, updated by events, rebuildable by replay.

What would need to change at scale

  • Orchestrator: if the flow adds partial shipments, split payments, or multiple compensation paths, choreography becomes unmanageable and an orchestrator is introduced.
  • Partitioning: the event stream is partitioned by order_id, so the per-order order is preserved and the consumers scale with the number of orders.
  • Tiered storage: the event stream's retention grows with the audit requirement; tiered storage keeps it affordable.
  • Read model sharding: the order view is sharded by user_id for user queries and by order_id for support queries; one of them is denormalized.
  • Multi-region: the write model is regional (users are pinned to a region); the event stream is regional with cross-region replication for the read model and the global view.
Interview Framing

Say: "Order service writes the order and outbox in one transaction, payment is synchronous because the user needs the result, and inventory, shipping, notification, and the read model are async consumers of the events. The outbox makes the DB-queue boundary exactly-once; the provider idempotency key makes the charge safe; the consumers dedup by order ID. If the flow grows complex, I'll add an orchestrator." That names every component, the consistency model per step, and the evolution path.
