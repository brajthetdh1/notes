Part A — Visibility Metrics and tracing, before anything breaks

1

What metrics do you monitor for a messaging system? How do you detect consumer lag, broker saturation, and rebalance storms?

Short Answer

Monitor four layers: broker health (under-replicated partitions, disk, network, request queues), consumer health (lag, rebalance rate, poll latency, commit rate), producer health (throughput, error rate, retry rate, batch size), and end-to-end (message latency, DLQ depth). The single most important metric is consumer lag; the single most alarming is under-replicated partitions.

The four layers

LayerCritical metricsWhat they reveal
Broker health Under-replicated partitions, offline partitions, active controller count, disk usage, ISR shrink/expand rate, request queue depth, network throughput Durability risk, capacity risk, cluster instability
Consumer health Consumer lag per partition, rebalance rate, poll latency, commit rate, max poll interval overruns Processing capacity, group stability, slow consumers
Producer health Throughput, error rate, retry rate, batch size, request latency p99, buffer exhaustion Upstream issues, backpressure, configuration problems
End-to-end Produce-to-consume latency p50/p99, DLQ depth, duplicate rate, schema validation failures User-facing impact, correctness, contract violations

Consumer lag — the primary signal

Lag is the difference between the producer's latest offset and the consumer's committed offset, per partition. It is the clearest indicator of whether consumers are keeping up. Three patterns matter:

  • Stable lag: the consumer processes at the same rate as the producer. Healthy.
  • Growing lag: the consumer is falling behind. Either the consumer is slow, the producer spiked, or a partition is stuck. Investigate immediately.
  • Lag approaching retention: the consumer is about to lose messages (the log will drop them). This is a data-loss risk and must be a page.

Lag should be monitored per consumer group, per topic, and per partition. The per-partition max is often more useful than the sum — a group with a healthy average may have one partition with massive lag, indicating a hot partition or a stuck consumer on that partition. Alert on the max, not the mean.

Under-replicated partitions

A partition whose ISR is smaller than its replication factor. This means a replica is behind or down. A transient value is normal (after a broker restart); a sustained non-zero value is a durability risk. If the ISR shrinks to 1 and the leader fails, the partition is unavailable (or data is lost if unclean leader election is enabled). Alert on any sustained non-zero value, and page on it growing or persisting beyond the expected recovery window.

Rebalance storms

A rebalance occurs when a consumer joins or leaves a group. A rebalance storm is when rebalances cascade: a slow consumer misses its heartbeat, is declared dead, triggers a rebalance, the rebalance redistributes its partitions, the new consumers take longer to catch up, miss their heartbeats, and the cycle repeats. The group makes no progress and lag grows.

The detection metrics:

  • Rebalance rate: rebalances per minute per group. A stable group has zero; a storm has several per minute.
  • Partition assignment churn: how often partitions move between consumers.
  • Consumer poll interval overruns: consumers that exceed max.poll.interval.ms are the usual cause; monitor the p99 poll interval.
  • Group state transitions: how often the group moves between PreparingRebalance, CompletingRebalance, and Stable.

The fix is per the cause: increase max.poll.interval.ms for slow batches, reduce max.poll.records, use cooperative sticky rebalancing to reduce the blast radius, and static group membership to avoid rebalances on restart.

Broker saturation metrics

A broker is saturated when any of these is high:

  • Request handler idle: the fraction of time the request handler threads are idle. Below 30% means the broker is CPU-bound.
  • Network processor idle: below 30% means the network threads are the bottleneck.
  • Log flush latency: rising latency means the disk is the bottleneck.
  • Request queue depth: growing queue means the broker is falling behind.
  • Disk usage: above 75% is a risk; above 90% is a page (the broker may stop accepting writes).

Producer metrics

The producer is often overlooked. Its metrics reveal upstream issues: a rising error rate means the broker is rejecting writes (quota, auth, ISR); a rising retry rate means the broker is slow or flaky; a rising batch size means the producer is accumulating messages (which is fine if intended, problematic if it is a symptom of backpressure). The producer's buffer exhaustion metric (how often the internal buffer fills) is a leading indicator of publish latency problems.

End-to-end latency

The single best user-facing metric is produce-to-consume latency: the timestamp of the message in the consumer minus the timestamp when the producer sent it. It captures the broker's delivery latency, the consumer's queue wait, and the consumer's processing time. A rising p99 means the user-visible delay is growing, even if the individual metrics look healthy.

DLQ depth and age

The DLQ depth is a correctness metric: a growing DLQ means messages are failing and accumulating for review. The DLQ age (oldest message) is even more important: a DLQ with 100,000 messages that are all 3 minutes old is a transient spike; a DLQ with 100 messages that are 3 weeks old is a silent failure (no one is processing the DLQ). Alert on both depth and age.

The dashboard

A standard messaging dashboard has four panels: broker health (under-replicated, offline, controller), consumer health (lag per group per partition, rebalance rate), producer health (throughput, error rate, retry rate), and end-to-end (latency, DLQ depth/age). Each panel is split by topic and by consumer group, so a problem with one topic or one group is visible rather than averaged away.

Common Pitfall

Monitoring the average consumer lag. A group with one stuck partition and nine healthy partitions has a low average lag but a severe problem. The per-partition max is the metric that catches it, and it must be alerted on separately from the aggregate.

Interview Framing

Say: "I'll monitor four layers: broker health, consumer health, producer health, and end-to-end. The primary signal is consumer lag — per partition, not averaged — and the primary alarm is under-replicated partitions. Rebalance rate and DLQ age catch the subtler failures." That names the layers and the two metrics that matter most.

How do you trace a message end-to-end through a distributed pipeline? What is the role of correlation IDs and distributed tracing?

Short Answer

Every message carries a trace context — a trace ID and span information — that flows through every producer, broker, and consumer. Each hop creates a span, and the spans are collected into a trace. The correlation ID (a business-level identifier) is separate from the trace ID: the trace is for the technical path, the correlation is for the business workflow. Both are needed.

Why tracing is hard for messaging

In a synchronous request, the trace is straightforward: the request carries the trace context, and each service creates a span. In messaging, the producer publishes and returns; the consumer picks up later, possibly on a different host, after a delay. The trace context must travel in the message itself, and the consumer must extract it and continue the trace. If the context is not in the message, the consumer starts a new trace and the link is lost.

The trace context in messages

The standard (W3C Trace Context) is a traceparent header with the trace ID, span ID, and sampling flags. For Kafka, this goes in the message headers (not the value); for SQS/RabbitMQ, in the message attributes. The producer injects it; the consumer extracts it and creates a new span as a child of the producer's span.

ComponentSpanAttributes
Producersendtopic, partition, key, message size
Brokerstore, replicateoffset, replica count, latency
Consumerprocessgroup, partition, offset, processing time
Consumer downstreamcallservice, endpoint, latency

The correlation ID

The trace ID identifies the technical path; the correlation ID identifies the business workflow. A user's order might involve ten messages across five topics, each in its own trace (if they are separate operations) or in one trace (if they are causally chained). The correlation ID — usually the order ID, or a business-generated request ID — links them across traces and across systems. It is what the support team searches for when a user reports a problem.

The correlation ID is propagated as a message attribute, a header, or a field in the payload. It is set at the entry point (the user's request) and carried through every message that stems from it. When a customer complains about order 12345, the support team searches the logs for the correlation ID and sees the full workflow, even if it spans multiple traces.

The trace's shape for a message pipeline

  1. User request — the API creates a trace; the trace ID is the root.
  2. Producer span — the service that publishes the message creates a child span.
  3. Broker span — the broker's store and replicate operations are children of the producer.
  4. Consumer span — the consumer's processing is a child of the producer's span (not of the broker's, because the consumer's work is caused by the message).
  5. Downstream calls — the consumer's calls to other services are children of the consumer's span.
  6. Fan-out — if the message triggers multiple consumers, each gets its own child span of the producer's span; the trace shows the fan-out as parallel branches.

Sampling

At high volume, tracing every message is impossible. The two strategies:

  • Head-based sampling: a fraction (1–10%) of traces are sampled at the root, and all spans in those traces are recorded. Simple, but slow or error traces may not be sampled.
  • Tail-based sampling: all spans are collected, and the sampling decision is made at the end (keep all slow or error traces, sample the successful ones). Catches the interesting traces, at the cost of a collector that buffers spans.

For messaging, head-based sampling with a higher rate (10%) plus tail-based for errors is the pragmatic combination. The trace context includes the sampling flag, so the decision is made once and propagated.

What the trace reveals

  • Where time is spent: producer publish latency, broker store latency, consumer processing latency, downstream call latency. The slowest span is the bottleneck.
  • Whether the message reached the consumer: if the producer span exists but no consumer span, either the consumer is slow/down or the message was lost.
  • The fan-out pattern: how many consumers processed the message, and which took the longest.
  • The end-to-end latency: the difference between the producer's start and the last consumer's end.

What tracing does not replace

Tracing shows the technical path; it does not show the business state. A trace of a payment might show that every hop succeeded in 200 ms, while the ledger entry is wrong because the consumer applied the delta twice. Correctness requires the dedup table's audit (Section 6), the outbox's records (Section 8), and the event log's content — not just the trace's timing. Tracing is for "why is it slow" and "where did it fail"; the business audit is for "what actually happened".

Interview Framing

Say: "Trace context flows in the message headers — traceparent for the technical trace, correlation ID for the business workflow. The producer injects, the broker stores, the consumer extracts and continues. The trace shows the latency and the path; the correlation ID links the business workflow across multiple traces." That names the mechanism, the two identifiers, and their distinct roles.

Part B — Failure Modes Poison pills, outages, and multi-tenancy

What is a poison message? How does it block a partition, and how do you prevent it from stopping the whole pipeline?

Short Answer

A poison message is a message that the consumer cannot process — a malformed payload, a missing field, an unsupported version, a business rule that always fails. If the consumer retries it indefinitely, it blocks the partition (head-of-line blocking) and no subsequent message is processed. The fix is a bounded retry count followed by a dead-letter queue (DLQ), so the poison message is removed from the pipeline and the others can proceed.

How the block happens

In an ordered queue or a Kafka partition, messages are processed in order. The consumer reads message 42, fails, retries, fails again, retries again. It does not commit the offset, so it does not advance to message 43. The partition is stuck on 42. Every retry consumes resources (CPU, database connections) and produces nothing. The lag grows, and the partition serves no useful work.

If the consumer is a Kafka consumer in a group, this affects only the partition with the poison message; the other partitions continue. But if the topic has one partition (or the poison message is the only partition), the whole topic stops. And even in a multi-partition topic, a poison message per partition eventually stops everything.

The bounded retry + DLQ pattern

  1. The consumer attempts to process the message. On failure, it retries with backoff (in-memory, up to N attempts).
  2. If the retries fail, the consumer publishes the message to a DLQ (or a retry queue with a delay) and commits the offset for the original message. The partition advances.
  3. The DLQ is a separate topic/queue with its own retention and monitoring. It holds the messages that could not be processed for review.
  4. A separate process (or a human) inspects the DLQ, fixes the underlying issue (schema, code, business rule), and re-drives the messages (or discards them if they are genuinely invalid).

The key property is that the consumer commits the offset after moving the message to the DLQ, so the partition is unblocked. The message is not lost — it is in the DLQ — but it no longer blocks the pipeline.

The retry policy matters

A poison message is not a transient failure. Retrying it 100 times is useless and harmful. The retry policy must distinguish:

  • Transient errors (network blip, database timeout): retry with backoff, likely to succeed.
  • Permanent errors (malformed payload, invalid enum, unsupported version): no retry, go directly to DLQ.
  • Business rule errors (order references a deleted user): no retry, DLQ for business review.

The consumer must classify the error, not blindly retry. A malformed message retried five times wastes five times the resources and delays the DLQ by the retry duration. The correct behaviour is to detect the permanent error (schema validation, deserialization failure) and skip the retries entirely.

Dead-letter queue inspection

The DLQ is only useful if someone inspects it. The operational design must include:

  • Alerting: DLQ depth and age are monitored; a growing or aging DLQ is a page.
  • Inspection tooling: a way to view messages, see the error, and categorize them.
  • Re-drive tooling: a way to move messages back to the main queue after a fix, with idempotency (the consumer dedups by message ID).
  • Discard policy: a way to permanently delete messages that are genuinely invalid, with an audit record.

The DLQ's own poison problem

A poison message in the DLQ that also fails its DLQ processing (if the DLQ has a consumer) blocks the DLQ the same way. The fix is the same: bounded retries and a second-level DLQ (a "parking lot" for messages that cannot be processed even by the DLQ's handler). Most systems do not need this — the DLQ is a store, not a processing queue — but if the DLQ has automated redrive, it must handle its own poison messages.

Prevention

The best poison message is the one that never happens:

  • Schema validation at produce time: the producer validates against the schema registry before publishing; malformed messages never reach the topic.
  • Schema validation at consume time: the consumer validates the message against the schema and rejects (to DLQ) rather than crashing.
  • Backward-compatible schemas: the schema registry enforces compatibility, so a schema change does not produce messages the consumer cannot read.
  • Forward-compatible consumers: the consumer ignores unknown fields and handles missing optional fields with defaults, so a schema addition does not break it.
  • Test data in staging: the producer's test suite publishes to a staging topic, and the consumer's test suite consumes it, catching schema mismatches before production.
Trade-off

The retry count and the DLQ threshold are a trade-off: too few retries and transient failures become DLQ messages (noise); too many and the partition is blocked for the retry duration. The right policy is short in-memory retries for transient errors, immediate DLQ for permanent errors, and a backoff retry queue for errors that are likely to resolve with time (dependency down for maintenance).

Common Pitfall

A DLQ without a consumer or an alert. The poison message is moved aside, the pipeline continues, but nobody knows. Weeks later, someone notices that 50,000 messages have accumulated and the downstream data is incomplete. The DLQ must have an owner, an alert, and a runbook from day one.

What happens when the message broker itself becomes unavailable? How does the system degrade, and how does it recover?

Short Answer

Producers buffer locally or fail fast (depending on configuration); consumers stop consuming and their lag grows; the system must degrade gracefully rather than fail completely. The recovery is: broker restarts or fails over, producers reconnect and flush their buffers, consumers resume from their last committed offsets, and the lag is drained. The design must ensure no data is lost during the outage and no thundering herd on recovery.

The outage scenarios

ScenarioDurationImpact
Single broker crashSecondsPartitions on that broker are unavailable briefly; leader election recovers
Broker restart (deploy)Seconds to minutesRolling restart; partitions migrate as brokers restart
Cluster-wide outage (power, network)Minutes to hoursAll publishing and consuming stops; the system is offline
Metadata/controller outageMinutesTopic creation, leader election, config changes fail; existing partitions continue
Disk fullMinutes to hoursBroker stops accepting writes; producers fail; consumers continue from existing log

Producer behaviour during the outage

Producers can be configured to:

  • Buffer in memory: the producer's internal buffer holds messages until the broker is reachable. The buffer is bounded; when full, the producer blocks (or drops, depending on block.on.buffer.full). For short outages (seconds), buffering works. For long outages (minutes), the buffer fills and the producer blocks.
  • Fail fast: the producer returns an error immediately; the upstream must decide whether to retry or drop. For non-critical messages (analytics, logs), dropping is acceptable. For critical messages (orders, payments), the upstream must buffer durably (e.g., in the outbox) and retry.
  • Write to a local durable queue: the producer writes to a local store (the outbox table) and a relay publishes when the broker is reachable. This is the most robust — the message survives the producer restart and the broker outage.

Consumer behaviour during the outage

Consumers cannot fetch from the broker, so they stop consuming. The lag metric grows (the producer's offset is ahead, but the consumer cannot advance). The consumer's health check should fail, so the load balancer stops routing traffic to it (if it serves HTTP as well). The consumer must not repeatedly retry against a dead broker; the client library handles reconnection with backoff.

The cascade risk

The broker outage is dangerous because it can cascade:

  • Upstream backpressure: the producers' buffers fill, the producers block, the upstream services slow down or fail. A broker outage becomes an API outage.
  • Downstream starvation: the consumers have nothing to consume, so the downstream services that depend on them (read models, notifications) stop updating.
  • Lag accumulation: the messages that were published before the outage accumulate as lag; when the broker recovers, the consumers must process the backlog, which can take hours and overload the consumers and their downstream dependencies.

The degradation strategy

  • Buffer critical messages durably. The outbox (Section 8) holds messages that the producer could not publish. When the broker recovers, the relay publishes them. No data is lost.
  • Drop non-critical messages. Analytics, logs, and telemetry are dropped if they cannot be published; the business continues.
  • Degrade the user-facing features. If the broker is down, the features that depend on it (real-time notifications, read models) are degraded. The core transaction (placing an order) may still work if it does not depend on the broker synchronously.
  • Rate-limit the recovery. When the broker recovers, the producers and consumers reconnect with jitter and the consumers process the backlog at a controlled rate. Without rate-limiting, the backlog flood can overwhelm the consumers and their downstream dependencies.

The recovery sequence

  1. Broker recovers. The controller stabilizes, the ISR catches up, the leaders are elected.
  2. Producers reconnect. The client library reconnects with backoff, and the buffered messages are flushed. Jitter prevents all producers from reconnecting at once.
  3. Consumers reconnect. The client library reconnects, rejoins the group, and resumes from the last committed offset. The lag is now the full backlog.
  4. Backlog processing. The consumers process the backlog. If the backlog is large (hours of messages), the consumers may need to be scaled up, or the downstream dependencies may need to be rate-limited to prevent overload.
  5. Steady state. The lag returns to normal, the backlog is drained, and the system is healthy.

What breaks during recovery

  • Reconnect storm: thousands of producers and consumers reconnect simultaneously. Jitter and connection rate limits are essential.
  • Backlog flood: the consumers process hours of messages in minutes, overloading their databases and downstream services. Rate-limited consumption (a max messages per second) prevents this.
  • Duplicate processing: the consumers re-process messages that were processed but not committed before the outage. Idempotency (Section 6) makes this harmless.
  • Partition imbalance: the recovery may concentrate load on the partitions that were offline longest. The lag per partition reveals this, and the consumer scaling must be per-partition.

The broker's own high availability

The broker is the single point of failure for the whole messaging system, and its availability is the foundation. The design must include:

  • Multi-AZ replication: brokers in at least three availability zones, so a zone failure does not lose data.
  • Replication factor 3: each partition has three copies, so a broker failure is tolerated.
  • Automatic failover: the controller promotes a new leader within seconds.
  • Regular chaos tests: kill a broker, fail a zone, and verify the cluster recovers (Question 6).
  • Backup and restore: for cluster-wide disasters, the broker's data must be backed up (or the messages must be recoverable from the outbox).
Trade-off

Buffering critical messages durably (outbox) gives no data loss at the cost of latency and complexity. Failing fast gives low latency and simplicity but loses messages during an outage. The right choice depends on the message's importance: critical messages get the outbox, non-critical messages fail fast and are dropped.

How do you design a multi-tenant messaging system? How do you isolate tenants' throughput, storage, and failures so one noisy tenant does not affect the others?

Short Answer

The isolation options are: shared everything (one topic per tenant, shared cluster), shared cluster with quotas (one cluster, per-tenant quotas and rate limits), or dedicated infrastructure (a cluster per tenant or per tier). The choice depends on the tenant count, the isolation requirement, and the operational budget. The key is to enforce isolation at every layer: topic naming, partition assignment, rate limits, quotas, and failure domains.

The three isolation models

ModelIsolationOperational costUse case
Shared cluster, shared topic Weak — a tenant's messages are mixed with others Lowest Multi-tenant SaaS where tenants are small
Shared cluster, per-tenant topic Moderate — each tenant has its own topic(s) Moderate Most multi-tenant systems
Shared cluster, per-tenant quotas Strong — rate limits and quotas per tenant Higher Systems with a few large tenants and many small ones
Dedicated cluster per tenant Strongest — total isolation Highest Enterprise tenants with regulatory or compliance requirements

Shared cluster, per-tenant topic

Each tenant gets its own topic (or set of topics): tenant_123.orders, tenant_456.orders. The tenant's messages are on their own partitions, with their own consumers. A tenant with high volume grows its topic's partitions, and the cluster's partition capacity is shared but the messages are not. The isolation is moderate: a tenant's consumer cannot see another tenant's messages (topic ACLs), but the cluster's resources are shared, so a high-volume tenant can consume partitions and broker CPU.

The per-tenant topic is the pragmatic default because it provides logical isolation at moderate cost. The naming convention (tenant prefix) makes the isolation visible and enforces it via ACLs.

Quotas

Kafka's quotas (client quotas and user quotas) limit a tenant's produce and consume rate and their bandwidth. The quota is enforced at the broker: a tenant that exceeds its quota is throttled (the broker delays its requests). The quotas prevent one tenant from saturating the cluster's network or CPU.

The quota design:

  • Per-tenant quota: each tenant has a produce/consume rate limit based on their subscription tier.
  • Burst allowance: a tenant can burst above the quota for a short period, then is throttled. This handles legitimate spikes without giving a tenant unlimited capacity.
  • Per-client quota: within a tenant, each producer/consumer has a quota, so one client cannot monopolize the tenant's allowance.
  • Monitoring: the throttle rate per tenant is a metric; a tenant that is constantly throttled needs more quota (or is being abusive).

Partition assignment

A shared cluster's partitions are assigned to brokers. If one tenant's topics dominate the partition count, they dominate the broker's resources. The mitigation is to limit the partitions per tenant (a quota on topic and partition creation) and to spread partitions across brokers evenly. Some managed Kafka services offer "isolation groups" or "broker racks" that assign different tenants to different broker sets, which is a middle ground between shared and dedicated.

Consumer group isolation

A tenant's consumers are in their own consumer group (with a tenant prefix), so they never compete with another tenant's consumers for partitions. The group coordinator still manages all groups, but the partitions are disjoint (each tenant's topics are separate), so there is no contention on the partition level.

Storage isolation

Each tenant's messages are stored in their topics. The retention policy is per-tenant: a tenant with strict compliance can have longer retention, a tenant with cost sensitivity can have shorter. The storage cost is attributable per tenant, which is needed for billing.

Failure isolation

A tenant's failure — a misbehaving producer publishing garbage, a consumer that crashes repeatedly, a tenant that hits a poison message — must not affect other tenants. The isolation mechanisms:

  • Topic-level: a broken consumer affects only its own consumer group and its own topic; the other tenants' topics are unaffected.
  • Rate limits: a tenant that floods the cluster is throttled by its quota, protecting the other tenants.
  • DLQ per tenant: the poison messages go to the tenant's DLQ, not a shared one.
  • Alerting per tenant: the health metrics are split by tenant, so a failing tenant is visible without affecting the others.

The noisy-neighbour problem

Even with per-tenant topics and quotas, a tenant can affect others through shared resources:

  • Broker CPU: a high-volume tenant's requests consume the broker's CPU, slowing the others. Quotas help, but a tenant below the quota can still consume a significant share. The isolation groups or dedicated brokers address this.
  • Network bandwidth: a tenant with large messages consumes the cluster's network. Quotas and message size limits help.
  • Disk I/O: a tenant with high write volume consumes the disk's IOPS. The broker's disk is shared, and a tenant that fills it affects all. Retention per tenant and disk quotas (where available) address this.
  • Controller metadata: a tenant that creates many topics/partitions slows the controller, which affects all metadata operations. Topic and partition quotas address this.

Dedicated clusters

For enterprise tenants with strict isolation (compliance, security, performance), a dedicated cluster is the only real isolation. The cost is high (the cluster is provisioned for one tenant), but the isolation is total: the tenant's messages are on their own brokers, their failures do not affect anyone, and their performance is guaranteed by their own capacity.

The standard progression: start with a shared cluster and per-tenant topics, add quotas as tenants grow, move the largest tenants to isolation groups or dedicated clusters. The design should support all three from day one, so the migration is possible without rearchitecting.

Common Pitfall

Sharing a topic across tenants with a tenant_id field in the message. It works until one tenant's messages dominate the partition, one tenant's consumer blocks the shared consumer group, or one tenant's retention requirement conflicts with another's. The per-tenant topic is the right default, even though it uses more topics.

Interview Framing

Say: "Per-tenant topics with ACLs, per-tenant quotas on produce and consume, and isolation groups or dedicated clusters for the largest tenants. The progression is shared cluster → quotas → dedicated, chosen by the tenant's size and compliance needs." That names the model, the enforcement, and the escalation path.

Part C — Verification & Operations Testing, runbooks, capacity, and lifecycle

6

How do you test a messaging system for correctness under failure? What is chaos engineering's role?

Short Answer

Test at three levels: unit tests for the consumer's idempotency, integration tests for the pipeline's end-to-end behavior, and chaos experiments for the system's behavior under failure. Chaos engineering continuously injects failures (broker kill, consumer crash, network partition, clock skew) in staging and verifies that the system recovers and no data is lost or duplicated.

Level 1 — Unit tests for idempotency

The consumer's idempotency is the foundation of correctness. It must be tested directly:

  • Same message twice: process the same message twice and assert the state is identical to processing it once.
  • Duplicate dedup key: insert the same dedup key twice and assert the second insert fails (or is skipped) and the business change is not applied twice.
  • Retry after partial failure: simulate a crash between the business write and the offset commit; on retry, assert the message is skipped and the offset is committed.
  • Concurrent duplicates: process the same message from two threads/instances and assert the dedup table's atomic insert prevents both from applying the effect.

Level 2 — Integration tests

The pipeline is tested end-to-end with a real broker (or an in-memory broker that mimics the semantics):

  • Happy path: a message is produced, consumed, and the business state is correct.
  • Consumer crash: the consumer crashes after processing but before committing; on restart, it re-processes and the effect is applied once.
  • Rebalance: a second consumer joins; the partitions are reassigned and the messages are processed exactly once.
  • Poison message: a malformed message is produced; the consumer sends it to the DLQ and continues with the next message.
  • Broker restart: the broker is restarted mid-processing; the consumer resumes from the last committed offset and the effect is correct.

Level 3 — Chaos engineering

Chaos engineering is the systematic injection of failures in a controlled environment, with the goal of finding weaknesses before they cause outages. For messaging, the experiments are:

ExperimentInjectsVerifies
Broker killKill one brokerLeader election completes; producers and consumers reconnect; no data loss
Broker pausePause a broker (SIGSTOP)Slow broker is detected; ISR shrinks; the leader fails over
Consumer killKill a consumer mid-batchRebalance; no lost messages; duplicates are deduplicated
Network partitionPartition a broker from the clusterSplit-brain prevention; ISR behavior; client retries
Consumer pausePause a consumer (slow batch)Rebalance timeout; partition reassignment; lag recovery
Clock skewShift a broker's clockTimestamps and ordering remain correct (or the failure is detected)
Disk fullFill a broker's diskBroker stops accepting writes; producers fail over; no corruption
Provider outageKill the downstream dependencyConsumer retries, DLQ, backpressure; recovery on restore

The chaos engineering process

  1. Define the steady state: the normal lag, latency, and throughput. This is the baseline against which experiments are judged.
  2. Form a hypothesis: "If I kill one broker, the cluster recovers within 60 seconds with no data loss."
  3. Inject the failure: in staging first, then in production with a small blast radius (one broker, one tenant, one topic).
  4. Observe: does the steady state return within the hypothesis? Does the alert fire? Does the runbook work?
  5. Fix or document: if the experiment revealed a weakness, fix it (or document the degraded behavior). If not, run a harsher experiment.
  6. Automate: run the experiments continuously (daily or weekly) to catch regressions.

What chaos engineering reveals

  • Undetected failures: a broker that is slow but not dead may not trigger the health check, and the cluster silently degrades.
  • Broken runbooks: the documented recovery steps may not work with the current tooling.
  • Alert gaps: the failure that the experiment injected may not have triggered any alert, meaning a real incident would be invisible.
  • Duplicate amplification: a consumer restart may cause a burst of duplicates that the dedup table was not designed to handle at that rate.
  • Cascade paths: the broker failure may reveal that the upstream service is not properly buffered and the API also fails.

The correctness invariants

The experiments must verify the invariants, not just the recovery time:

  • No lost messages: every message that the producer acknowledged is eventually processed. Verify by counting produced vs consumed, or by a checksum of the message set.
  • No duplicate effects: each message's effect is applied once. Verify by a canary message with an idempotency key that the consumer records, or by a state check (e.g., the account balance matches the sum of the successful charges).
  • Ordering preserved: for per-key ordered topics, the events for a key are applied in order. Verify by a per-key sequence check.
Common Pitfall

Running chaos experiments only in staging. Staging does not have the production traffic, the data distribution, or the failure timing. The most valuable experiments are in production with a small blast radius (one broker, one consumer group), where the real behaviour is observed.

How do you test a messaging pipeline for correctness? What is the difference between testing the transport and testing the consumer?

Short Answer

Test the transport (broker, serialization, delivery semantics) separately from the consumer (business logic, idempotency, ordering). The transport is tested with integration tests against a real broker; the consumer is tested with unit tests for the logic and property-based tests for idempotency and ordering. Together, they verify that the pipeline is correct end-to-end.

Testing the transport

The transport is the broker and its client libraries. Its correctness is mostly the broker's job (tested by the broker's maintainers), but the integration must be verified:

  • Serialization round-trip: produce a message, consume it, assert the payload is identical. Test with schema evolution (old schema, new schema, missing optional field).
  • Delivery guarantee: produce with acks=all, kill a broker, verify the message survives and is consumed. Produce with idempotence enabled, retry the producer, verify no duplicate.
  • Offset management: produce 100 messages, consume 50, commit, restart the consumer, verify it resumes at 51 and re-processes nothing before 51 (or re-processes with dedup).
  • Rebalance: start with 2 consumers and 4 partitions, kill one consumer, verify the remaining consumer takes all 4 partitions and processes the messages in order (per partition).
  • Retention: produce a message, advance the retention window, verify it is gone (and the consumer fails with OffsetOutOfRange if it tries to read it).

Testing the consumer logic

The consumer's business logic is tested in isolation:

  • Unit tests: the message → effect transformation. Given a message, assert the database state, the output message, or the API call.
  • Idempotency tests: the same message processed twice produces the same effect. Given a message and a dedup table with the key already present, assert the business change is not applied.
  • Ordering tests: given a sequence of messages with the same key, assert the effects are applied in order. Given a sequence with a gap, assert the consumer buffers or fails correctly.
  • Error handling: given a malformed message, assert the consumer sends it to the DLQ and continues. Given a transient error, assert the consumer retries with backoff.
  • Concurrency: given two messages processed by two threads, assert the dedup table prevents double application (or the transaction serializes them).

Property-based testing

Property-based testing generates random inputs and asserts the invariants. For messaging, the properties are:

  • Idempotency: processing a message N times is equivalent to processing it once, for any message and any N.
  • Commutativity: for messages that are commutative (state assignments), the order does not matter. For messages that are not (increments), the order matters and the consumer must preserve it.
  • No loss: every produced message is eventually consumed (given enough time and no retention expiry).
  • Convergence: after a series of messages and a series of failures, the state is the same as if the messages were processed once, in order, with no failures.

The property-based framework generates random messages, injects random failures (crash after processing, crash before commit, rebalance), and asserts the final state matches the expected. This is the most rigorous test and catches bugs that unit tests miss.

The end-to-end test

The full pipeline — producer, broker, consumer, downstream — is tested with a small dataset and a real broker (Testcontainers or an embedded broker). The test asserts:

  • The message is produced and consumed.
  • The business effect is correct.
  • After a broker restart, the effect is still correct (no duplicate, no loss).
  • After a consumer crash, the effect is still correct.

The end-to-end test is the integration test that catches the interaction bugs between the components. It is slower than the unit tests, so it runs in CI on every merge (or every few hours), not on every local change.

The canary message

A canary message is a message with a known payload and a known expected effect, produced periodically (every minute) in production. A monitor consumes it and asserts the effect is correct. If the canary fails, the pipeline is broken and the alert fires before the users notice. The canary tests the pipeline's health without waiting for real traffic to reveal the problem.

What testing misses

No test can cover every failure mode. The tests are the safety net; chaos engineering (Question 6) and production monitoring (Question 1) are the live verification. The combination — unit tests for logic, integration tests for the pipeline, chaos for the failure modes, monitoring for the production behavior — is the only way to be confident.

Interview Framing

Say: "I test the transport separately from the consumer. The transport tests cover serialization, delivery guarantees, offsets, and rebalances against a real broker. The consumer tests cover the business logic, idempotency, and ordering, with property-based tests for the invariants. The end-to-end test covers the interaction, and a canary message in production verifies the pipeline is alive." That names the layers and the tools.

What are the most common messaging incidents, and what does the runbook look like for each?

Short Answer

The most common incidents are: consumer lag growing, a poison message blocking a partition, a rebalance storm, a broker failure, a disk full, and a DLQ overflowing. Each has a distinct symptom and a short, well-rehearsed runbook. The runbook is what the on-call engineer follows at 3 AM, and it must be tested, not just written.

The incident catalog

IncidentSymptomFirst response
Consumer lag growing Lag metric rising for one or more partitions Check consumer CPU, downstream latency, partition skew; scale consumers if possible
Poison message One partition's lag grows while others are normal; repeated errors in consumer logs Identify the offset, inspect the message, move to DLQ (or skip), unblock the partition
Rebalance storm Frequent rebalances; lag grows; group never stable Check poll interval overruns, increase max.poll.interval, reduce batch size, enable static membership
Broker down Under-replicated partitions spike; leader elections; producer errors Verify failover completed; check client reconnection; watch for cascade
Disk full Broker rejects writes; producers fail; disk usage at 100% Reduce retention, clean up old segments, add disk, or expand the cluster
DLQ overflowing DLQ depth and age growing Inspect messages, categorize errors, fix the root cause, redrive or discard
Schema incompatibility Schema registry rejects a producer; messages not published Identify the incompatible change, roll back or fix the schema, republish
Consumer offset lost Consumer resets to earliest or latest; massive reprocessing or gap Identify the cause (retention expiry, offset topic loss), restore from a known offset, notify downstream

Runbook: consumer lag growing

  1. Confirm the lag is growing, not transient. Look at the trend over 5–10 minutes. A transient spike during a traffic burst is normal.
  2. Identify the scope. One partition, one topic, one consumer group, or all? Per-partition lag reveals hotspots.
  3. Check consumer health. CPU, memory, GC pauses, network. A saturated consumer needs scaling (if partitions allow) or optimization.
  4. Check downstream latency. The consumer may be waiting on a slow database or API. That is the actual bottleneck, and adding consumers makes it worse.
  5. Check for poison messages. If one partition's lag is growing and the consumer logs show repeated errors at a specific offset, it is a poison message (next runbook).
  6. Check for rebalance storms. If the group is unstable, that is the cause (next runbook).
  7. Scale if possible. Add consumers up to the partition count. If the downstream is the bottleneck, do not scale — rate-limit or fix the downstream.
  8. If the lag approaches retention, the consumer will lose data. This is a page; the mitigation is to increase retention temporarily (if the cluster has disk) or accept the loss and reset the consumer.

Runbook: poison message

  1. Identify the offset. The consumer logs show the offset that is failing repeatedly. The partition and the message content are in the log.
  2. Inspect the message. What is wrong with it? Malformed JSON, missing field, invalid enum, business rule? The error message usually says.
  3. Decide: skip or fix. If the message is genuinely invalid (garbage data), skip it: move it to the DLQ (or use a tool to advance the offset past it). If the message is valid but the consumer has a bug, fix the consumer and redeploy.
  4. Unblock the partition. The message must be moved to the DLQ (or the offset advanced) so the partition can continue. Use the DLQ tool or the broker's offset tool.
  5. Verify the partition advances. The lag should start decreasing.
  6. Post-incident: add validation at produce time or consumer time to prevent this class of message from reaching the pipeline.

Runbook: rebalance storm

  1. Confirm the storm. Rebalance rate is high (several per minute), the group is never Stable, and lag is growing.
  2. Check poll interval overruns. The consumer logs show "consumer poll timeout" or "member kicked out of group". The batch is taking longer than max.poll.interval.ms.
  3. Reduce the batch size. Set max.poll.records lower (e.g., from 500 to 100) so each batch processes faster.
  4. Increase the poll interval. Set max.poll.interval.ms higher (e.g., from 5 min to 10 min) if the processing is legitimately slow.
  5. Enable static membership. Set group.instance.id so a restarting consumer rejoins without a rebalance.
  6. Use cooperative sticky rebalancing. Set partition.assignment.strategy=CooperativeStickyAssignor so rebalances are incremental.
  7. Rolling restart the consumer group after the config change, one instance at a time.

Runbook: broker down

  1. Confirm the failover. The controller should elect a new leader within seconds. The ActiveControllerCount must be exactly 1; the under-replicated partitions should be recovering.
  2. Check client reconnection. Producers and consumers should reconnect to the new leader automatically. If they are not, check the client logs and DNS.
  3. Watch for cascade. If the producers' buffers fill and the upstream services slow down, the broker outage is becoming an API outage. The mitigation is to shed load or buffer durably.
  4. Restart the failed broker. The broker should rejoin and catch up its replicas. Monitor the ISR recovery.
  5. Do not force an unclean leader election. If the failed broker held the only in-sync replica, the partition is unavailable — but promoting an out-of-sync replica loses data. Wait for the broker to return (or accept the data loss explicitly).

Runbook: disk full

  1. Identify the broker. The disk usage metric shows which broker is full.
  2. Reduce retention temporarily. Lower retention.ms or retention.bytes on the largest topics, so the broker can delete old segments.
  3. Delete old segments manually if the retention change does not free space quickly enough. Use the broker's log management tools; do not delete files directly (it corrupts the broker's state).
  4. Move partitions. If the disk cannot be freed, reassign some partitions to other brokers (Kafka's partition reassignment tool).
  5. Add disk or brokers. The long-term fix is more capacity. Track the growth rate and plan for it.

Runbook: DLQ overflowing

  1. Inspect the messages. Group by error type: malformed (garbage), missing field (schema mismatch), business rule (invalid reference).
  2. Fix the root cause. If it is a schema mismatch, fix the producer. If it is a business rule, fix the data. If it is a consumer bug, fix the code.
  3. Redrive the fixable messages. After the fix, move the affected messages from the DLQ back to the main topic, with idempotency (the consumer dedups).
  4. Discard the unfixable messages. Some messages are genuinely invalid (garbage from a buggy producer). Delete them with an audit record.
  5. Monitor the DLQ age after the fix, to confirm it is draining.

The runbook's properties

  • Tested: the runbook is exercised in game days (Question 6); the first time the on-call engineer runs it should not be during a real incident.
  • Specific: the commands, the tools, and the dashboards are named; not "investigate the issue".
  • Bounded: the response time is bounded (e.g., "if the lag is not recovering in 15 minutes, escalate").
  • Rollback-aware: each step has a rollback if it makes things worse.
  • Updated: the runbook is updated after every incident with what actually happened.
Common Pitfall

A runbook that says "escalate to the Kafka team". If there is no Kafka team (or they are asleep), the incident stalls. The runbook must have steps the on-call engineer can execute without a specialist, or an explicit escalation with the specialist's on-call rotation.

How do you plan capacity for a messaging backbone? What metrics drive the sizing, and how do you forecast growth?

Short Answer

Capacity is driven by four metrics: throughput (MB/s), partition count, connection count, and retention (storage). Each has a per-broker limit, and the cluster is sized for the peak, not the average. The forecast is based on traffic growth (expected increase per quarter), and the plan includes headroom (N+1 for failure, 2× for burst).

The four capacity dimensions

DimensionWhat it measuresPer-broker limit
ThroughputBytes/s in and outNetwork (1–25 Gbps), disk (100–500 MB/s)
Partition countNumber of partitions per broker2,000–10,000 (depends on version, KRaft raises it)
Connection countProducers + consumers + admin clients10,000–50,000 per broker
RetentionBytes stored on diskBroker disk size (TB per broker)

Throughput sizing

The throughput is the peak produce rate plus the peak consume rate (they are separate directions on the network). For a topic at 100 MB/s produce with 3 consumers, the produce traffic is 100 MB/s per broker's share, and the consume traffic is 300 MB/s. The broker's network interface must handle both, plus the replication traffic (3× the produce rate for replication factor 3).

The per-broker throughput budget:

  • Network: 1 Gbps ≈ 125 MB/s, 10 Gbps ≈ 1.25 GB/s. The cluster needs enough brokers to keep the network utilization below 70%.
  • Disk: sequential write throughput of an SSD is 500 MB/s–3 GB/s; the replication multiplies this. A broker with 3× replication and 100 MB/s produce writes 300 MB/s to disk.
  • CPU: serialization, compression, and TLS consume CPU. The request handler idle metric reveals the headroom.

Partition sizing

Each partition is a unit of replication, a file handle, and a controller metadata entry. The per-broker limit is 2,000–10,000 partitions (KRaft raises it to hundreds of thousands). The cluster's total partition count is the sum across topics. A cluster with 500 topics × 20 partitions = 10,000 partitions, which needs at least 2–5 brokers (at 2,000–5,000 per broker).

The partition count also affects rebalance time and leader election time. A broker with 10,000 partition leaders that fails triggers 10,000 leader elections, which can take minutes. The capacity plan should keep the per-broker partition count moderate, not at the maximum.

Connection sizing

Each producer and consumer holds at least one connection per broker. A client with 100 producers and 1,000 consumers generates 1,100 connections, spread across brokers. The broker's connection limit is usually 10,000–50,000, but the file descriptor limit (ulimit) must be raised to match.

Retention sizing

Storage = throughput × retention × replication factor. A topic at 100 MB/s with 7 days retention and 3× replication is 100 MB/s × 604,800 s × 3 ≈ 181 TB. The cluster's disk must be at least 1.5× this (for headroom and compaction).

The retention is often the largest capacity item. Reducing retention from 30 days to 7 days cuts storage by 77%. Tiered storage (Section 3, Question 11) moves old data to object storage, making the hot tier's retention a small fraction of the total.

Peak vs average

The cluster is sized for the peak, not the average. A system with 20 MB/s average and 100 MB/s peak needs the capacity for 100 MB/s. The peak-to-average ratio depends on the workload: user-facing chat is 3–5×, batch processing is 10–100×, event-driven systems with scheduled campaigns can be 100×+.

The peak is measured, not assumed. The historical peak (the max over the last 30 days) plus a growth factor (the expected increase) is the sizing input. The capacity plan should be reviewed quarterly and updated with the actual growth.

Headroom

The cluster must have headroom for:

  • Failure (N+1): losing one broker must not saturate the rest. With N brokers at 60% utilization, losing one pushes the rest to 60% × N/(N-1) = 67% (for N=10), which is acceptable. At 80% utilization, losing one pushes to 89%, which is not.
  • Burst: a traffic spike (a campaign, a breaking news event) must be absorbed without degradation. The headroom is the burst ratio (peak/expected) plus a margin.
  • Recovery: a broker restart triggers replica catch-up, which consumes network and disk. The headroom must cover the catch-up traffic.

The standard is 30–40% headroom at steady state. That is expensive (you are paying for 30–40% idle capacity), but it is the cost of resilience.

Forecasting growth

The forecast is based on:

  • Historical growth: the throughput and storage growth per quarter over the last year. A 20% quarterly growth compounds to 2× in a year.
  • Business forecast: the product team's planned features and the expected user growth. A new feature that doubles the event rate is a known capacity hit.
  • Seasonal peaks: Black Friday, end of quarter, product launches. The capacity plan must account for the known peaks.

The capacity plan is a spreadsheet (or a dashboard) with the current utilization, the growth rate, the projected utilization at 3, 6, and 12 months, and the scaling action (add brokers, reduce retention, tiered storage) for each threshold. The thresholds: 70% utilization triggers planning, 80% triggers action, 90% triggers an emergency.

The scaling action

When the cluster approaches capacity, the actions are:

  • Add brokers: increases throughput and partition capacity. The rebalance moves partitions to the new brokers, which is an online operation but takes hours.
  • Reduce retention: frees disk immediately. The trade-off is less history for replay.
  • Enable tiered storage: moves old data to object storage, making the hot tier's retention small. The trade-off is cold-read latency.
  • Reduce replication factor: from 3 to 2 frees 33% of the disk but reduces durability. Not recommended for critical topics.
  • Archive to a data lake: copy old topics to S3/Parquet and delete from Kafka. The trade-off is that replay requires the lake, not Kafka.

Cost

The capacity is a cost. The broker fleet is the largest line item (compute + storage + network). The plan must balance:

  • Retention: more retention costs more disk; less retention costs more replay capability.
  • Replication: 3× replication costs 3× storage; 2× costs 2× but loses the ability to survive 2 broker failures.
  • Headroom: 40% headroom costs 40% more; 10% headroom costs less but risks saturation.
  • Tiered storage: object storage is 5–10× cheaper than broker disk; the trade-off is cold-read latency.
Common Pitfall

Sizing for the average and assuming autoscaling will handle the peak. Kafka does not autoscale — adding brokers is a manual (or scheduled) operation that takes hours. The peak must be provisioned in advance, and the autoscaling (if any) is for consumers, not brokers.

How do you manage the data lifecycle in a messaging system — retention, archival, and deletion?

Short Answer

The lifecycle has three phases: hot (on the broker, fast, retained for days to weeks), warm (in object storage, slower, retained for months), and cold (archived, cheapest, retained for years). Each phase has a different storage medium, access latency, and cost. The retention policy per topic determines when data moves between phases, and the deletion policy determines when it is permanently removed (for compliance and cost).

The three tiers

TierStorageAccessRetentionCost
HotBroker disk (SSD)Milliseconds0–30 daysHighest
WarmObject storage (S3 Standard)Seconds30 days – 1 yearLow
ColdGlacier / archiveMinutes to hours1–10 yearsLowest

Hot retention

The hot retention is the messages on the broker's disk, served by the consumer in real time. It is sized for the consumer's catch-up window (how long a consumer can lag before losing messages) plus a margin. A consumer that might be down for a weekend needs at least 3 days of hot retention; a consumer that is expected to be up always needs less.

The hot retention is set per topic (retention.ms and retention.bytes). The trade-off is storage cost vs catch-up capability. For most topics, 7–14 days is the sweet spot.

Warm retention

When the hot retention expires, the data is either deleted (if no warm tier) or moved to object storage (tiered storage, Section 3, Question 11). The warm tier serves older messages to slow consumers, new consumer bootstraps, and replay for bug fixes. The access is slower (seconds instead of milliseconds) but the cost is a fraction of the broker disk.

Kafka's native tiered storage (KIP-405) implements the warm tier: the broker uploads closed segments to S3 and removes them from local disk, keeping the hot window on the broker. The consumer reads from S3 transparently when it requests an old offset.

Cold archival

For compliance, audit, and long-term analytics, the messages are copied to a cold archive (S3 Glacier, an HDFS data lake, or a warehouse). The archive is not part of the Kafka cluster; it is a separate store with its own query interface (Athena, Spark, BigQuery). The archival is done by a consumer that reads the topic and writes to the archive, or by Kafka Connect S3 sink.

The cold archive is for "we might need it someday" — audit, legal, ML training. It is not for operational reads; the latency is minutes to hours, which is fine for a quarterly audit and useless for a chat app.

Deletion

The deletion policy is the last phase. Data must eventually be deleted:

  • Cost: keeping everything forever is expensive, even in cold storage.
  • Compliance: GDPR's right to erasure requires deleting personal data on request; retention policies must have a maximum period.
  • Data hygiene: old data that is no longer needed is a liability (security, confusion).

The deletion mechanisms:

  • Log retention: the broker deletes segments older than the retention window. This is the hot tier's deletion.
  • Log compaction: the broker keeps only the latest value per key. This is a different policy (for changelogs, not event streams).
  • Tiered storage expiration: the object storage lifecycle policy deletes objects after the warm retention window.
  • Archive lifecycle: the cold storage's lifecycle policy moves data to Glacier or deletes it after the compliance period.
  • Right to erasure: a request to delete a user's data requires finding all messages containing that user's data and deleting them (or writing tombstones). This is hard in an append-only log, and the design must account for it (encrypt per-user with a key, delete the key).

The GDPR problem

An append-only log with a user's data cannot be modified in place. The "right to be forgotten" requires deleting the data, which means either:

  • Tombstones: write a deletion marker for the user's messages; the consumer applies the deletion. The original message is still in the log until retention expires, which may violate the regulation.
  • Crypto-shredding: encrypt each user's data with a per-user key; delete the key to make the data unreadable. The ciphertext remains in the log, but it is undecipherable, which satisfies the regulation in most interpretations.
  • Rebuild without the user's data: replay the log into a new topic, skipping the user's messages. This is expensive and complex.

Crypto-shredding is the pragmatic solution for messaging systems with strict privacy requirements. The per-user key is stored in a KMS, and deleting it is a single operation. The log's messages for that user are unreadable, which satisfies the erasure requirement without modifying the log.

The retention policy per topic

The retention is per topic, not global. The policy is set by the topic's owner and reviewed periodically:

Topic typeHotWarmCold
Real-time events (orders)14 days1 year7 years (compliance)
User activity (analytics)7 days90 days2 years
Logs / telemetry3 days30 daysNone
Changelogs (compacted)Infinite (compacted)——

The policy is a business decision, not a technical one. The technical design implements it (the retention configuration, the tiering, the archival pipeline, the deletion mechanism). The business owns the "how long" and the compliance owns the "must delete".

The lifecycle pipeline

  1. Produce to the topic (hot tier).
  2. Hot retention (7–14 days): the broker serves the consumer; old segments are closed.
  3. Tiering (if enabled): closed segments are uploaded to object storage and removed from the broker. The consumer can still read them (slower).
  4. Warm retention (object storage lifecycle): objects are retained for the configured period, then transitioned to cold or deleted.
  5. Archival (for compliance): a separate pipeline copies the topic to the archive (S3 Glacier, data lake) with the required retention.
  6. Deletion: the object storage lifecycle deletes the warm objects; the archive lifecycle deletes the cold objects; the crypto-shredding deletes the per-user keys (for erasure).

Monitoring the lifecycle

The lifecycle is invisible until it fails. The metrics:

  • Tiering lag: how far behind the tiering is (the oldest segment not yet uploaded).
  • Object storage cost: the total bytes in S3, growing as expected.
  • Deletion success: the objects deleted per lifecycle run; a failure means the lifecycle policy is broken.
  • Retention compliance: the oldest message in each topic matches the retention policy; a longer retention is a compliance violation.
Common Pitfall

Assuming that Kafka's retention deletes the data. It deletes the hot data, but the warm tier (if configured) and the archive (if a separate pipeline) have their own retention. The lifecycle is a chain, and each link must have an explicit deletion policy. An archive that is never deleted is an infinite cost.

Interview Framing

Say: "Three tiers: hot on the broker for 7–14 days, warm in object storage for months to a year, cold in archive for compliance. The retention is per topic, set by the business. Deletion is by log retention, tiered storage lifecycle, and archive lifecycle — and crypto-shredding for GDPR erasure." That names the tiers, the policies, and the special case.
