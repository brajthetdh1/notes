Part A — Requirements → Database Design Turning a product brief into a physical schema

1

How do functional requirements influence schema design?

Short Answer

Functional requirements define what the system must remember and enforce. The nouns become entities, the verbs become relationships or event tables, and the business rules become constraints. A schema that does not encode the invariants of the domain will leak data corruption into the application layer.

Functional requirements are the sentences in a product brief that describe behaviour: “a customer can place an order containing many products”, “an order can be cancelled only before it ships”, “a user may belong to several organisations”. Every one of these sentences has a structural consequence. The job of the designer is to run a disciplined translation from prose to tables.

Step 1 — Extract nouns as candidate entities

Read the requirements and underline every noun that has an identity and a lifecycle: customer, product, order, invoice, shipment. Nouns that are merely descriptive (“red”, “express”) are usually attributes or lookup values, not entities. The critical test is: does this thing need to be referenced by other things, and does it change over time independently? If yes, it becomes a table with a primary key.

Step 2 — Extract verbs as relationships or events

Verbs reveal how entities connect and whether the connection is a static link or a time-stamped fact. “A customer places an order” is a relationship with a foreign key. “A customer views a product” is an event — it happens repeatedly, it has a timestamp, and its volume is orders of magnitude larger than orders. Modelling events as rows in the same table as state is one of the most common and most expensive design mistakes, because it destroys index locality and makes every read scan through history.

Step 3 — Turn business rules into constraints

Requirements like “emails must be unique”, “an order total must be non-negative”, “a seat cannot be booked twice for the same showing” are integrity rules. Each one should be enforced at the lowest reliable layer — ideally the database — using UNIQUE, CHECK, FOREIGN KEY, and EXCLUDE constraints. Application-only enforcement fails the moment two requests race, a batch job runs, or a second service writes the same table.

Step 4 — Model the lifecycle as state

Almost every domain object has a state machine: pending → paid → shipped → delivered → refunded. The requirement implicitly defines both the allowed states and the legal transitions. A status column plus a status_history table captures both the current truth and the audit trail. If the business later asks “when did this order ship?”, a design without history cannot answer, and retrofitting history into a high-volume table is painful.

Step 5 — Let reporting needs shape read structures

Requirements usually hide analytical questions: “show me revenue by region this quarter”, “list the top 10 best-selling products”. These are aggregate, wide-scan queries that fight with transactional row lookups. Recognising them early tells you to plan for materialized views, precomputed rollups, or a CDC pipeline to a warehouse — rather than discovering at month six that the OLTP database melts every time the dashboard refreshes.

Trade-off

Faithfully encoding every rule as a database constraint gives strong guarantees but increases coupling: constraint changes require migrations, and heavy constraint checking reduces write throughput. Many teams push soft rules to the application. The defensible line is: anything that causes money loss or data corruption if violated under concurrency belongs in the database.

Interview Tip

When given a prompt, restate three or four functional requirements explicitly, then say: “Each of these maps to a table, a relationship, or a constraint — let me show you.” Interviewers score the visible translation process, not just the final ER diagram.

How do non-functional requirements — latency, throughput, consistency, availability, durability — affect database choice and schema?

Short Answer

Non-functional requirements (NFRs) decide the class of database and the shape of the schema. Two systems with identical entities but different latency, consistency, or scale targets should end up with materially different physical designs. NFRs are where design becomes opinionated.

Functional requirements tell you what to store. Non-functional requirements tell you what you are allowed to do while storing it. This is the single most under-weighted part of schema design in interviews: candidates draw the same normalised five-table ER diagram whether the target is a 200-user internal tool or a 50-million-user consumer app. That is a red flag.

NFRDesign Consequence
Latency (p99 < 10 ms) Point-lookup access by primary key, covering indexes, denormalised read models, in-memory or SSD-resident working set, minimal joins on the hot path.
Throughput (100k writes/s) LSM-tree engine, batched writes, partitioned/append-only tables, few secondary indexes, no hot counter rows.
Strong consistency Single-leader relational database, normalized schema, real transactions, serializable or repeatable-read isolation.
Eventual consistency Denormalised documents, per-service data ownership, async replication, version columns for conflict resolution.
High availability (99.99%) Multi-AZ replicas, automatic failover, schema that tolerates read-from-replica lag (no read-after-write assumptions).
Durability (zero loss) Write-ahead log with synchronous commit, synchronous replication to a second node, tested PITR backups.
Security / compliance Column-level encryption, tokenised PII, row-level security policies, separate audit tables, data residency by region.
Cost efficiency Compression, columnar storage for analytics, tiered storage (hot/warm/cold), shorter retention on raw events.

Why consistency drives everything

Consistency requirements have the largest cascading effect. If the product demands read-your-writes — “after I post a comment I must see it immediately” — you cannot blindly route reads to asynchronous replicas, and you cannot rely on a multi-region active-active store without a coordination mechanism. If eventual consistency is acceptable, entire categories of design open up: denormalised copies, fan-out on write, cross-region replication, and document models that embed related data. Getting this wrong in either direction is costly — too strong and you have built an expensive, fragile system; too weak and users see phantom data.

Why durability is not the same as availability

A database can be highly available and still lose data: asynchronous replication to another node keeps the primary writable during a network partition, but a failover can silently drop the last few seconds of commits. If the NFR says “no committed order may be lost”, you need synchronous replication or a quorum commit, which directly increases write latency. This is a genuine trade-off, not a configuration detail — and a strong candidate should name it explicitly.

Deriving concrete design levers

  • Latency budget per query: subtract network, serialization, and application time from the end-to-end SLA; the remainder is the database budget, which dictates index and join strategy.
  • Peak-to-average ratio: a system with 20× daily peaks needs headroom and partitioning by time, not just a bigger instance.
  • Read/write ratio: 100:1 justifies read replicas and caching; 1:1 shifts the entire conversation to write path efficiency.
  • Retention window: a 30-day retention requirement allows aggressive TTL and time-based partition drops instead of expensive deletes.
Common Pitfall

Treating NFRs as “later optimisation”. Physical schema decisions — partition keys, index sets, embedding vs referencing — are extremely expensive to reverse once data volume reaches hundreds of millions of rows. NFRs must be inputs, not afterthoughts.

How do you translate access patterns into a schema? What is query-first schema design?

Short Answer

Query-first design starts from the list of queries the application will actually run, not from a tidy entity diagram. You enumerate each query with its filter, sort, and access shape, then design keys and indexes so that every critical query is served by a single, efficient access path.

Entity-first modelling asks “what are the things in my domain?”. Query-first modelling asks “what will the application ask for, how often, and how fast?”. Both produce tables, but the second produces tables that are fast. In relational databases query-first is a strong influence on indexes and sometimes on table boundaries. In wide-column and document databases it is mandatory — if a query is not supported by the primary key or a secondary index, it either does not work or scans the entire dataset.

The five-step process

  1. Enumerate the queries. Write them as a numbered list with an estimated frequency: “Q1: get user by ID (very high)”, “Q2: list a user’s last 20 orders, newest first (high)”, “Q3: revenue by region per month (low, analytical)”.
  2. Annotate the access shape. For each query note the equality filters, range filters, sort order, and whether it needs a join or an aggregate.
  3. Find the covering key. The ideal index matches equality columns first, then the range or sort column — giving a single index seek plus a small ordered scan, with no sorting step.
  4. Group queries by partition. Queries that always filter on the same column are candidates for co-location — a partition key, a table cluster, or a denormalised read table.
  5. Only then draw the entities, splitting or merging tables so each hot query has a natural home.

Worked example — a chat application

The dominant query is “fetch the last 50 messages in conversation X, oldest to newest, and page backwards”. That single sentence dictates everything: partition by conversation_id, cluster by message_time DESC (or use the message's sortable ID), and the entire read becomes one contiguous range scan with no global sort. A schema that partitioned messages by user_id would be unusable for the primary screen, no matter how clean the normal form.

Applying it in a relational database

You still normalise for correctness, but you let access patterns decide the index set and the read models. A composite index on (tenant_id, status, created_at DESC) exists purely because a specific dashboard query needs it. Equally, you may add a materialized view order_summary that is refreshed on write, because a join across four tables cannot meet the latency budget even with perfect indexes.

Trade-off

Over-fitting to today's queries creates brittle schemas: the moment product adds a filter, the index strategy collapses. The durable rule is to over-optimise the small set of high-frequency, low-latency queries and deliberately keep the rest flexible — accepting a slower path for rare, ad-hoc, or analytical access, often served from a separate read store.

Interview Tip

Say out loud: “Before I design the schema, let me list the top five queries and their frequency.” Then design keys against them. This one sentence signals senior-level thinking more than any diagram.

How do you design a schema for a read-heavy vs a write-heavy workload?

Short Answer

Read-heavy systems reward duplication: denormalise, precompute, add covering indexes, cache, and serve from replicas. Write-heavy systems reward minimalism: fewer indexes, narrow rows, append-only structures, and partitioning. The governing principle is that every index and every denormalised copy is a tax paid on writes.

Read-heavy design

A 100:1 read-to-write ratio (a product catalogue, a news site, a profile page) means read latency and read throughput dominate cost. The levers, in order of impact:

  • Denormalise aggressively. Store the author's display name on the post row so rendering a feed needs one lookup, not a join. Accept the write-time update cost, which occurs rarely.
  • Precompute aggregates. Keep like_count and comment_count as columns, maintained by the write path, rather than running COUNT(*) on every page view.
  • Materialized views and read models. Build a table that already matches the screen's shape. This is the heart of CQRS: separate write model, purpose-built read model.
  • Covering indexes. An index containing every column the query needs avoids a heap fetch entirely, often cutting latency by half.
  • Read replicas. Horizontal read scaling with the caveat of replication lag and the read-your-writes problem.
  • Cache layers. Cache entity-by-ID lookups with short TTLs and explicit invalidation on write.

Write-heavy design

Event ingestion, metrics, logging, and IoT telemetry are write-dominated. Here the enemy is write amplification: each secondary index, each foreign key check, and each unique constraint costs additional I/O per insert.

  • Strip secondary indexes. Keep only indexes that serve a genuinely hot read. Unused indexes can silently double or triple write cost.
  • Prefer append-only. Inserts into the end of a B-tree are cheap; random updates to scattered rows cause page splits and fragmentation. Append-only tables also compress far better.
  • Avoid hot counter rows. A single UPDATE counter SET v = v + 1 serialises all writers. Shard the counter into N rows, or accumulate deltas and fold them periodically.
  • Batch writes. Grouping 500 inserts per transaction amortises commit overhead dramatically — often a 5–10× throughput gain with no schema change.
  • Partition by time. Time-based partitions keep the active write set small, make old data droppable by partition, and reduce index height.
  • Consider LSM-tree engines (Cassandra, Scylla, RocksDB-based stores) which convert random writes into sequential appends — ideal for ingestion, at the cost of read amplification.

Mixed workloads

Most real systems are both. The pragmatic answer is separation: a normalised, index-light write model feeding denormalised read models via CDC or events. This keeps the write path fast and the read path shaped for the screen, at the cost of eventual consistency between them — a trade-off you must name explicitly.

Trade-off

Denormalisation is a bet that reads outnumber writes. If the ratio inverts — as it does during a backfill or a bulk import — the same schema becomes a liability. Design decisions should record the assumed ratio so it can be revisited when usage shifts.

How do you estimate data volume, growth rate, index size, and storage needs?

Short Answer

Sizing is arithmetic with explicit assumptions: rows per day × average row width → raw data; multiply by an index overhead factor (typically 20–50%); multiply again by replication factor; add backup and headroom. The value of the estimate is not precision — it is exposing which assumption dominates.

The core formula

Storage = (rows/day × bytes/row × retention days) × (1 + index overhead) × replication factor × (1 + headroom).

Worked example — chat application

Assume 10 million daily active users, each sending 40 messages per day.

  • Rows/day = 10M × 40 = 400M messages/day.
  • Row width: message_id (16 B UUID), conversation_id (16 B), sender_id (16 B), body (~200 B average), created_at (8 B), metadata (~60 B) ≈ 320 bytes. Add row overhead and page fill factor (~20%) → ~400 bytes.
  • Raw/day = 400M × 400 B ≈ 160 GB/day.
  • Retention of 1 year (hot 90 days, cold archive) → hot set ≈ 90 × 160 GB = 14.4 TB.
  • Index overhead: primary key index plus one secondary index on (conversation_id, created_at) ≈ +30% → ~18.7 TB.
  • Replication factor 3 → ~56 TB of cluster storage for the hot tier.
  • Headroom 40% for compaction, temporary space, and growth → ~78 TB.

Estimating index size

An index is not free. A B-tree index stores the indexed key columns plus a row pointer, plus internal nodes and fill-factor slack. A practical rule of thumb: a single-column index on a fixed-width key costs roughly 10–20 bytes per row; a composite index costs proportionally more. A table with four indexes can have an index footprint approaching its data footprint. In wide-column stores where the primary key is the storage layout, partitioning the data is not optional.

Working set vs total data

Total storage determines cost; the working set determines latency. If 95% of reads touch the last 7 days, then the memory requirement is sized on 7 days of data (~1.1 TB in the example above), not on 90. Caching and buffer-pool sizing should target the working set — this is often the highest-leverage number in the whole estimate.

Derived capacity figures

  • Write QPS = 400M / 86,400 ≈ 4,600 writes/s average. With a 3× peak factor → ~14,000 writes/s peak.
  • Read QPS: each message is read by roughly its conversation participants; assume 5× fan-out → ~23,000 reads/s average, ~70,000 peak. This drives the replica and cache count.
  • IOPS: at 16 KB average I/O, 14,000 writes/s × 16 KB ≈ 224 MB/s of write bandwidth before replication — a number that should immediately inform engine and disk choice.
Common Pitfall

Forgetting that deletes do not reclaim space immediately in LSM stores, and that updates create new versions. Tombstones and compaction temporarily increase disk usage. Size for at least 1.5× the steady-state figure on LSM-based systems.

How do you decide between OLTP-optimized and OLAP-optimized database designs?

Short Answer

OLTP serves many small, concurrent, point-access transactions and wants row-oriented storage, normalized schemas, and narrow indexes. OLAP serves few, large, scanning aggregations and wants columnar storage, denormalised star schemas, and compression. The decision follows from the access shape, not from the vendor.

The defining contrast

DimensionOLTPOLAP
Query shapePoint lookup, few rows, by keyFull/range scan, millions of rows, aggregated
ConcurrencyThousands of short transactionsDozens of long-running queries
Storage layoutRow-oriented (whole record together)Columnar (one column together)
SchemaNormalized (3NF/BCNF)Star / snowflake, heavily denormalised
IndexingMany narrow B-tree indexesFew indexes; partition pruning, zone maps, min/max metadata
WritesFrequent small inserts/updatesBulk batch loads, append-heavy
CompressionModestVery high (run-length, dictionary, delta)
Failure granularityIndividual transaction rollbackWhole-query retry

Why columnar wins for analytics

A columnar store reads only the columns a query touches. A query computing SUM(amount) GROUP BY region over a billion rows reads two columns instead of sixty — a 30× I/O reduction before compression even applies. Column values also share type and domain, so compression ratios of 5–20× are routine. Row stores, by contrast, must read entire pages to find one record, which is exactly right for “get order 12345”.

The cardinal sin: analytics on the OLTP database

Running dashboards against the transactional primary is a classic production incident waiting to happen. A single unindexed GROUP BY can hold locks, evict the working set from the buffer pool, and starve the writes that generate revenue. The standard remedy is CDC → warehouse: stream the write-ahead log to a columnar store, and let analysts query a copy.

Deciding in practice

  1. Characterise the query mix. If 95% of queries return fewer than 100 rows by key, it is OLTP. If they aggregate over time ranges, it is OLAP.
  2. Check concurrency and latency SLOs. Sub-10 ms point reads with thousands of concurrent users is an OLTP problem statement.
  3. Identify the reporting horizon. If reporting is a required feature, plan a separate analytical store from day one — retrofitting is far more expensive than provisioning it.
  4. Consider HTAP only with evidence. Hybrid systems genuinely serve some workloads, but they are usually a compromise on both axes; adopt them when the scale is modest or the freshness requirement is strict.
Interview Tip

Draw the boundary explicitly: “This table is the OLTP source of truth; the analytics path is a CDC stream into a columnar warehouse.” Naming the seam between the two is a strong senior signal.

Part B — Data Modeling Fundamentals From abstract domain to concrete tables

7

Explain conceptual, logical, and physical data models. How do they differ in practice?

Short Answer

The three models are three levels of abstraction over the same reality: conceptual answers what the business talks about, logical answers how those things relate precisely, and physical answers how they are stored on a specific engine. Each survives a different kind of change.

Conceptual model — the vocabulary of the business

Entities and relationships only. No attributes beyond the identifying ones, no data types, no keys, no technology. A conceptual model for a bookstore might be a single line: “Customers place Orders containing Books”. It exists to align stakeholders on scope and vocabulary, and it is deliberately technology-agnostic — it should still be true if the company migrates from PostgreSQL to DynamoDB.

Logical model — precise, technology-independent structure

Here entities gain full attribute lists, primary and foreign keys, cardinality and optionality, and normalisation is applied (usually to 3NF). Still no vendor-specific types or indexes. Continuing the example: Customer(customer_id PK, name, email UNIQUE), Order(order_id PK, customer_id FK, order_date, status), OrderLine(order_id FK, book_id FK, quantity, unit_price). The many-to-many between Order and Book has been resolved into an associative entity with its own attributes — a purely logical decision.

Physical model — the artefact you actually deploy

Now technology intrudes. customer_id becomes a BIGSERIAL or a UUID. status becomes an enum or a SMALLINT lookup. You choose the storage engine, add a composite index on (customer_id, order_date DESC) for the order-history screen, partition OrderLine by month, decide whether unit_price is NUMERIC(12,2) or integer minor units, and specify the fill factor and tablespace. Two databases with identical logical models can have completely different physical models and performance profiles.

Why the separation matters

  • Conceptual models survive product pivots. “Customers place Orders” remains true whether orders are stored relationally or as events.
  • Logical models survive technology changes. Moving from MySQL to PostgreSQL changes the physical layer only.
  • Physical models are disposable. They should be expected to change — that is the whole point of the lower layers being stable.
  • Review happens at different audiences. Business stakeholders review the conceptual model; engineers review the logical; DBAs and performance engineers review the physical.

The practical failure mode

Teams skip the logical layer. They jump from a whiteboard sketch straight to CREATE TABLE, and discover six weeks later that an entity had an unmodelled many-to-many, or that a “one-to-one” was actually optional. The logical layer is where cardinality and optionality are decided, and those decisions are the expensive ones to reverse.

Trade-off

Full three-layer modelling is expensive for small, well-understood domains. The pragmatic rule: model conceptually on a whiteboard, write the logical model down for anything with more than five entities or more than one team involved, and let the physical model emerge iteratively from real query patterns.

What is an ER model? How do you represent entities, attributes, relationships, and cardinality?

Short Answer

The Entity-Relationship model is a diagrammatic language for describing a domain in terms of things (entities), their properties (attributes), and how they are connected (relationships with cardinality and participation). It is a logical-model tool, and it maps to relational tables by a small set of mechanical rules.

Entities

An entity is a class of distinguishable objects about which we store data: Customer, Product, Invoice. A strong entity has its own identifier (an order_id exists independently). A weak entity cannot be identified without its owner — OrderLine is meaningless without an Order, and its identifier is the composite (order_id, line_no). Weak entities are drawn with a double rectangle and connect via an identifying relationship (double diamond).

Attributes

  • Simple — atomic, e.g. age.
  • Composite — decomposable, e.g. address → street, city, postcode.
  • Multivalued — several values per entity, e.g. phone_numbers. These violate 1NF and must become a separate table.
  • Derived — computable from others, e.g. age from date_of_birth. Drawn with a dashed ellipse; usually not stored.
  • Key attribute — underlined; uniquely identifies instances.

Relationships and cardinality

Cardinality expresses how many instances of one entity can be associated with instances of another: one-to-one (1:1), one-to-many (1:N), and many-to-many (M:N). It is drawn on the relationship line with crow's-foot or 1/N notation.

CardinalityPhysical MappingExample
1:1Foreign key on either side (or merge into one table if truly mandatory)User ↔ UserProfile
1:NForeign key on the "many" sideCustomer → Orders
M:NNew associative (junction) table with two foreign keysStudents ↔ Courses

Participation (optionality)

Cardinality says “how many can be related”; participation says “must they be”. A total-participation relationship (every Order must have a Customer) is drawn with a double line; partial participation (a Customer may have zero Orders) with a single line. This distinction maps directly to NOT NULL versus nullable foreign keys — and getting it wrong is a frequent source of either data corruption or awkward application-side checks.

Mapping ER to tables

  1. Each strong entity → a table with its key attribute as primary key.
  2. Each weak entity → a table whose primary key includes the owner's key.
  3. 1:N relationship → foreign key on the N side.
  4. M:N relationship → a junction table with a composite primary key of both FKs, plus any relationship attributes (e.g. enrolled_at, grade).
  5. Multivalued attribute → its own table.
  6. Composite attribute → either flattened into columns or normalised into its own table depending on usage.
Interview Tip

When you draw an ER diagram under time pressure, label cardinality and participation on every edge. Unlabelled edges are the most common deduction — they suggest you have not decided what the relationship actually means.
